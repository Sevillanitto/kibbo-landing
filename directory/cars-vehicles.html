Cars & Vehicles

Registration, VIN and history checks, recalls, dealer and repair disputes, insurance and accident claims, financing, and MOT/roadworthiness — for the US, UK, EU, and Australia.

← All free resources

Vehicle regulation is unusually fragmented, even by this directory's standards — the US and Australia run registration, licensing, and dealer complaints at the state/territory level with no national equivalent; the EU has no single vehicle registration or recall portal at all; and even within the UK, official government checks (DVLA, DVSA) sit alongside a separate world of paid commercial history providers that are not government services. This directory keeps those distinctions explicit — where a genuinely unified resource exists, it's linked directly; where it doesn't, a representative example is used and the variation is stated plainly.

Need a document instead? Our templates and generators cover a pre-purchase inspection, a dealer dispute, a repair dispute, a warranty claim, and an accident record, and our free checklists walk through the same ground step by step.

Section 1 · Vehicle & Driver Authorities

Register a vehicle or hold a driver's licence — the base-level authority in each region, referenced again later in this directory for title transfer, import, and roadworthiness.

Register a vehicle or driver's licence in the US (example: California)

California DMV — Department of Motor Vehicles dmv.ca.gov →

What: Vehicle registration and driver licensing are run entirely at the state level in the US — there is no single national DMV. California's DMV is shown here as a representative, well-documented example; check your own state's equivalent agency.

Register a vehicle or driver's licence in the UK

DVLA — Driver and Vehicle Licensing Agency gov.uk — Vehicle registration →

What: The DVLA handles vehicle registration and driver licensing across the UK, run as a single national agency (unlike the US and Australia).

Register a vehicle in Australia (example: NSW)

Service NSW — Vehicle Registrations service.nsw.gov.au — Vehicle registrations →

What: Vehicle registration and driver licensing are handled by each state/territory transport authority in Australia — there is no single national register. NSW is shown here as a representative example; check your own state or territory's equivalent (VicRoads in Victoria, TMR in Queensland, and so on).

The EU has no single vehicle registration authority

What: Each EU member state runs its own national vehicle registration system — there is no EU-wide registration body to link here. A vehicle generally must be registered in the owner's country of residence, regardless of where in the EU it was purchased.

Section 2 · Vehicle Consumer Protection

General consumer protection guidance specifically for vehicle purchases.

Understand your rights buying a used car in the US

FTC — Used Cars consumer.ftc.gov — Used cars →

What: Official FTC guidance on buying a used car — vehicle history reports, the Buyers Guide window sticker requirement, and checking for open recalls before you buy.

Get consumer guidance on a vehicle purchase in the UK

Citizens Advice — Consumer citizensadvice.org.uk/consumer →

What: General UK consumer rights guidance, applicable to vehicle purchases and disputes — the same reference used across other Kibbo directories for general consumer complaints.

Understand your consumer rights buying a vehicle in Australia

ACCC — Australian Competition and Consumer Commission accc.gov.au/consumers →

What: Australia's national consumer protection authority — the same reference used across other Kibbo directories, covering ACL consumer guarantee rights that apply to vehicle purchases too.

Resolve a cross-border EU vehicle purchase dispute

ECC-Net — European Consumer Centres Network eccnet.eu →

What: The same network referenced in other Kibbo directories, relevant here specifically for cross-border vehicle purchases between EU member states — a common scenario given how many used vehicles cross borders within the EU.

Section 3 · Vehicle History & VIN Checks

Official, typically free or low-cost government-backed checks for finance, theft, and salvage status — not a full history report substitute (see note below).

Check a US vehicle's official title and brand history

NMVTIS — National Motor Vehicle Title Information System vehiclehistory.gov →

What: The official, DOJ-backed system tracking title and brand history (salvage, flood, odometer). Consumer-facing history providers like Carfax, DMVDesk, and Experian sell reports built partly from NMVTIS data, but NMVTIS itself does not sell reports directly to individual consumers — it provides data only through its Approved Data Provider list, linked from vehiclehistory.gov.

Check if a US vehicle is reported stolen or salvage/flood-damaged

NICB VINCheck nicb.org/vincheck →

What: A free VIN check against records reported by participating insurers for theft or salvage/flood damage — run by the National Insurance Crime Bureau, a nonprofit. Limited to 5 free searches per VIN per user per 24 hours.

Check a UK vehicle's official DVLA record

DVLA — Vehicle Enquiry Service gov.uk — Get vehicle information from DVLA →

What: The official, free government starting point — tax status, MOT status, first registration date, and basic vehicle details, straight from DVLA. This is distinct from paid commercial history-check services like HPI, which check for outstanding finance and recorded write-offs that DVLA's own free service doesn't cover.

Check for outstanding finance on an Australian vehicle

PPSR — Personal Property Securities Register ppsr.gov.au →

What: The official Australian government register (run by AFSA) for checking outstanding finance or security interests registered against a vehicle by its VIN — a small search fee applies. Referenced again in Section 7 for the financing angle specifically.

Government checks aren't a full history report

What: A complete vehicle history report — full accident history, complete service records — generally comes from a commercial provider (Carfax, HPI, and similar) in every one of these markets, not a free government source. The entries above are the official, typically free or low-cost starting points specifically for finance, theft, and salvage/title status — genuinely useful, but not a substitute for a full paid history report if you need one.

Section 4 · Recalls & Vehicle Safety

Check whether a specific vehicle has an open safety recall.

Check for open US safety recalls by VIN

NHTSA — National Highway Traffic Safety Administration nhtsa.gov/recalls →

What: The official US vehicle safety recall lookup by VIN, also covering child car seats, tires, and equipment recalls.

Check for a UK vehicle, part, or accessory recall

GOV.UK / DVSA — Check if a vehicle, part or accessory has been recalled gov.uk — Check vehicle recall →

What: The official recall check, run on behalf of government by the Driver and Vehicle Standards Agency (DVSA), working with manufacturers and the DVLA.

The EU has no single central vehicle recall lookup

EU Safety Gate (formerly RAPEX) ec.europa.eu/safety-gate →

What: Responsibility for initiating a vehicle recall in the EU sits with the national type-approval authority that approved the vehicle — there is no single central EU vehicle recall database you can search by VIN. Safety Gate is the EU's public rapid-alert portal for dangerous products generally (including some vehicles), useful for browsing notifications, but it isn't a VIN-lookup recall tool. Check your specific national type-approval authority, or your manufacturer's own recall lookup, for a genuine VIN-level answer.

Check for an Australian vehicle recall

Vehicle Recalls (Australian Government) vehiclerecalls.gov.au →

What: The dedicated Australian government vehicle recall lookup — enter your vehicle's details to check for an active recall. A more specific tool than the general productsafety.gov.au site, which covers product safety and recalls broadly, not just vehicles.

Also check your manufacturer's own recall tool

What: Every major manufacturer runs its own VIN-based recall lookup tool separately from these government portals. Checking both — the government database and the manufacturer's own tool — is worth doing, since coverage and update timing can differ slightly between them.

Section 5 · Dealers, Garages & Repair Disputes

Escalate a dispute with a dealership or repair shop once direct contact hasn't resolved it.

Escalate a dealer or garage dispute in the UK

The Motor Ombudsman themotorombudsman.org →

What: A CTSI-approved automotive ADR body — but it can only help if the specific dealer or garage is accredited to one of its Codes of Practice (Vehicle Sales Code, Service and Repair Code, or Vehicle Warranty Products Code). If the business isn't a member, this route isn't available — go to Trading Standards or small claims instead.

File a dealer complaint in the US

Better Business Bureau — File a Complaint bbb.org/file-a-complaint →

What: Dealer licensing and complaint handling is state-regulated in the US, with no single national body — the BBB accepts and forwards complaints against a dealer nationally as a practical first step, while your state's own dealer licensing board (varies by state — e.g. a Secretary of State, Motor Vehicle Administration, or DMV division) handles formal licensing complaints.

File a dealer or repairer complaint in Australia (example: NSW)

NSW Fair Trading — Vehicle Complaint Form service.nsw.gov.au — Complaint about a motor vehicle dealer or repairer →

What: A free dispute resolution service for issues with a licensed motor dealer or repairer in NSW. Dealer complaint handling is state-based in Australia — NSW is shown as a representative example; check your own state or territory's fair trading equivalent.

The EU has no single automotive ombudsman

What: There is no EU-wide automotive dispute body. Dealer and garage complaints in the EU run through each country's own general consumer ADR bodies — see the ECC-Net reference in Section 2 for cross-border cases specifically.

Section 6 · Insurance & Accident Claims

Escalate a motor insurance dispute, or claim when the at-fault driver is uninsured or untraced.

File an insurance complaint in the US

NAIC — National Association of Insurance Commissioners content.naic.org/consumer →

What: Insurance regulation is state-based in the US — every state has its own Department of Insurance. NAIC's consumer portal routes you to your specific state's complaint page, since there's no single federal insurance regulator.

Escalate a motor insurance complaint in the UK

Financial Ombudsman Service financial-ombudsman.org.uk →

What: Handles motor insurance complaints once your insurer's own process hasn't resolved things — free to consumers, funded by regulated financial businesses.

Escalate a motor insurance complaint in Australia

AFCA — Australian Financial Complaints Authority afca.org.au — Insurance complaints →

What: Australia's independent, free external dispute resolution body for insurance disputes, including motor insurance. Decisions are binding on the insurer, not on you.

A representative national insurance ombudsman (EU example: Germany)

Versicherungsombudsmann (Germany) versicherungsombudsmann.de →

What: Insurance dispute resolution in the EU is handled at member-state level — there is no single EU-wide insurance ombudsman. Germany's state-approved Insurance Ombudsman is shown here as a representative, free consumer arbitration example; check your own country's national equivalent (for example, France's Médiateur de l'Assurance).

Claim against an uninsured or untraced driver in the UK

Motor Insurers' Bureau (MIB) mib.org.uk →

What: A real, statutory compensation mechanism (funded by every insured driver's premiums) for accidents caused by uninsured or untraced (hit-and-run) drivers. In the US, this is handled instead through each state's own uninsured/underinsured motorist coverage rules — check your state insurance department (above) rather than a single national body. In Australia, a comparable role is played by each state's own nominal defendant scheme.

Claim against an uninsured or unidentified driver in Australia (example: NSW)

Nominal Defendant (NSW) — administered by SIRA sira.nsw.gov.au — Nominal Defendant →

What: NSW's scheme covering injury claims where the at-fault vehicle was unregistered/uninsured or couldn't be identified (a hit-and-run). This is state-based in Australia — NSW is shown as a representative example; check your own state's equivalent nominal defendant scheme.

Section 7 · Financing, Loans & Vehicle Ownership

Vehicle finance guidance, and checking for outstanding finance before you buy.

Understand vehicle financing rules in the US

CFPB — Auto Loans consumerfinance.gov — Auto loans →

What: Federal consumer guidance on shopping for and understanding an auto loan, including a shopping sheet for comparing offers apples-to-apples.

Check for outstanding finance before buying in Australia

PPSR — Personal Property Securities Register ppsr.gov.au →

What: Same register referenced in Section 3, relevant here specifically for the financing angle — check before you buy to avoid an outstanding-finance repossession risk, since a car with unpaid finance can be repossessed even after you've bought it in good faith.

Understand vehicle finance complaint rules in the UK

FCA — Car Finance Complaints fca.org.uk — Car finance complaints →

What: Official FCA guidance on car finance complaints, including the current motor finance commission redress scheme (covering finance agreements from 6 April 2007 to 1 November 2024) — note this scheme was partially suspended in mid-2026 pending a legal challenge, so check the current status before relying on a specific timeline.

Vehicle repossession guidance

CFPB — What happens if my car is repossessed? consumerfinance.gov — Vehicle repossession →

What: Official US guidance on what happens after a repossession, including deficiency balances and your rights during the process. UK readers should use the Citizens Advice reference in Section 2 for the equivalent guidance on vehicle repossession and hire purchase.

Title and ownership documents vary by jurisdiction

What: Title/ownership document requirements and transfer processes vary significantly by country, and by state within the US and Australia. Rather than listing every variation here, use the Section 1 authority for your specific jurisdiction as the starting point for title transfer questions.

Section 8 · Vehicle Legal & Administrative Resources

Roadworthiness, emissions, and traffic/parking penalty disputes. Registration/title transfer and import/export requirements are handled by the Section 1 authorities above, not duplicated here.

Check a UK vehicle's MOT history

GOV.UK — Check the MOT History of a Vehicle gov.uk — Check MOT history →

What: Free official record of every MOT test since 2005 — pass/fail, mileage, and advisory notes — by registration number.

Book or understand a UK MOT test

GOV.UK — Getting an MOT gov.uk — Getting an MOT →

What: Official guidance on when an MOT is required and how the test works — required from the third anniversary of a vehicle's registration, then annually. The MOT test itself includes an emissions component (see the emissions entry below).

Check roadworthiness/safety certificate requirements in Australia (example: NSW)

NSW Government — Roadworthiness nsw.gov.au — Roadworthiness →

What: NSW requires a periodic roadworthy/safety certificate ("Pink Slip") for registration renewal on vehicles over 5 years old. This varies significantly by state — some Australian states and territories don't require periodic inspections at all. NSW is shown as a representative example; check your own state's specific rules.

Emissions testing requirements, by market

California DMV — Smog Check Program (US example) dmv.ca.gov — Smog inspections →

What: Emissions testing rules vary by market and, in the US, by state — California's biennial Smog Check Program is shown as a representative example. In the UK, emissions testing is a component of the MOT test itself (see above), not a separate process. In the EU, emissions checks are part of the Periodic Technical Inspection under Directive 2014/45/EU, applied nationally by each member state.

Challenge a parking ticket in the UK

GOV.UK — Parking Tickets gov.uk — Parking tickets →

What: The general GOV.UK overview on challenging a Penalty Charge Notice — you generally have 28 days to challenge, and the specific process is still run by the individual local council or authority that issued it.

Dispute a parking ticket in the US (example: New York City)

NYC Department of Finance — Parking and Camera Violations nyc.gov — Parking and camera tickets →

What: Parking and traffic ticket disputes are handled at the city or county level in the US — NYC is shown as a representative example, where disputes are actually heard by a separate hearings agency (OATH), not the Department of Finance itself. Check your own city or county's specific process.

Request a review of a traffic or parking fine in Australia (example: NSW)

Service NSW — Request a Review for a Fine service.nsw.gov.au — Request a review for a fine →

What: Fine review requests are handled by each state's own revenue/fines body in Australia — NSW's Revenue NSW process is shown as a representative example; check your own state or territory's equivalent.
