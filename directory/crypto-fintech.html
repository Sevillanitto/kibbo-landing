Crypto & Fintech

Who regulates it, how to check if an exchange is actually licensed, and where to report a scam or a frozen account.

← All free resources

Crypto regulation is fragmented by design — no single body licenses exchanges globally, and "regulated" can mean very different things depending on the country and the specific activity. These are the official sources, organized by function, not blended together.

Worried about a specific opportunity? Start with our free Crypto Scam Red Flags Checklist.

Section 1 · Crypto Regulators

Licensing and conduct regulators with actual jurisdiction over crypto activity — not scam-reporting portals (see Section 8 for that).

US — Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)

U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission investor.gov →

What: Federal jurisdiction over crypto assets that qualify as securities. sec.gov itself blocked automated verification from this environment on every attempt (HTTP 403, including with browser headers) — investor.gov is the SEC's own official investor-education domain and was used instead since it returned a verified 200. Re-verify sec.gov directly from a different network if it's specifically needed here.

US — Commodity Futures Trading Commission (CFTC)

U.S. Commodity Futures Trading Commission cftc.gov →

What: Federal jurisdiction over crypto assets that qualify as commodities, including derivatives and futures markets.

US — Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN)

FinCEN fincen.gov →

What: AML enforcement and money services business (MSB) registration — the body an exchange operating in the US must register with for anti-money-laundering purposes, distinct from the SEC/CFTC's securities/commodities jurisdiction.

UK — Financial Conduct Authority (FCA)

Financial Conduct Authority fca.org.uk →

What: The UK's cryptoasset AML/CTF registration authority, and the body building the incoming FSMA-based crypto regime (applications open 30 September 2026).

EU — European Securities and Markets Authority (ESMA)

ESMA esma.europa.eu →

What: Coordinates consistent application of MiCA (Regulation (EU) 2023/1114) across the EU and publishes the EU-wide register of authorised Crypto-Asset Service Providers (CASPs). ESMA does not itself license firms — that's delegated to each member state's own National Competent Authority (NCA) below.

Germany — BaFin

Federal Financial Supervisory Authority bafin.de →

What: Germany's National Competent Authority for MiCA licensing.

France — AMF

Autorité des marchés financiers amf-france.org →

What: France's National Competent Authority for MiCA licensing.

Spain — CNMV

Comisión Nacional del Mercado de Valores cnmv.es →

What: Spain's National Competent Authority for MiCA licensing.

Italy — CONSOB

Commissione Nazionale per le Società e la Borsa consob.it →

What: Italy's National Competent Authority for MiCA licensing.

Finland — FIN-FSA

Finanssivalvonta (Financial Supervisory Authority) finanssivalvonta.fi →

What: Finland's National Competent Authority for MiCA licensing.

Ireland — Central Bank of Ireland

Central Bank of Ireland centralbank.ie →

What: Ireland's National Competent Authority for MiCA licensing — a common EU domicile for large crypto firms passporting services across the bloc.

Australia — ASIC

Australian Securities and Investments Commission asic.gov.au →

What: Australia's conduct and licensing regulator for crypto assets that fall within its financial services jurisdiction (e.g. AFS-licensed platforms) — distinct from AUSTRAC's AML-specific role below.

Australia — AUSTRAC

Australian Transaction Reports and Analysis Centre austrac.gov.au →

What: Australia's AML/CTF regulator — digital currency exchange providers must register with AUSTRAC specifically for anti-money-laundering purposes, separate from ASIC's broader licensing/conduct role.

Section 2 · Licensed Exchanges by Country

Naming specific exchanges as "licensed" goes stale fast and this session's tools can't query these interactive registers directly (they require search-by-entity-name, not a fetchable list) — so rather than guess which exchanges currently hold authorization, these are the four official lookup tools themselves. Search the exchange's exact legal entity name (which often differs from its brand name — e.g. Coinbase operates in the UK as CB Payments Ltd, Kraken as Payward Ltd) directly against the regulator, not a third-party summary site.

US — FinCEN MSB Registrant Search

FinCEN msb.fincen.gov →

What: Search whether an exchange is registered as a Money Services Business. FinCEN explicitly states registration is not an endorsement — verify the business independently as well.

UK — FCA Financial Services Register

Financial Conduct Authority register.fca.org.uk →

What: Search by firm name or reference number and look for "Cryptoasset activities" under registered permissions. The FCA also separately maintains a public warning list of unauthorised firms.

EU — ESMA CASP Register

ESMA esma.europa.eu — MiCA register →

What: The EU-wide register of Crypto-Asset Service Providers authorised under MiCA Article 109, published by ESMA. An authorisation from any one member state's NCA passports across the whole EEA and appears in this register.

Australia — ASIC Professional Registers

ASIC ASIC Professional Registers search →

What: Search for an Australian Financial Services (AFS) licence directly, for platforms whose activities fall under ASIC's licensing jurisdiction.

Section 4 · Crypto Tax Software

Tools that import exchange/wallet history and calculate gains, losses and income for tax filing. Coverage varies by country — check before assuming your jurisdiction is supported. See also our free Crypto Tax Records Checklist for what to keep track of year-round regardless of which tool you use.

Koinly

Koinly koinly.io →

Country support: Markets itself broadly across the US, UK, Canada, Australia and 20+ other countries with locally-formatted tax reports — confirm your specific country's report format is supported before relying on it for filing.

CoinTracker

CoinTracker cointracker.io →

Country support: Strongest for US filing (IRS forms), with support for several other countries — confirm your specific country's report format is supported before relying on it for filing.

CoinLedger

CoinLedger coinledger.io →

Country support: US-focused (IRS forms) with reports for a number of other countries — confirm your specific country's report format is supported before relying on it for filing.

Recap

Section 6 · Proof of Reserves

Whether an exchange's reserve claims are independently verifiable varies significantly and changes over time — this reflects each exchange's own published methodology as of this writing, not an independent Kibbo audit. Verify an exchange's current claim directly on its own site before relying on it, and treat any "proof of reserves" claim as a point-in-time snapshot, not a continuous guarantee.

Kraken

Kraken Proof of Reserves kraken.com — proof of reserves →

What: One of the longest-running PoR programs in the industry (since 2014), publishing quarterly attestations with published methodology and third-party accounting-firm involvement. Its cryptographic-proof approach lets technically sophisticated users independently verify claims rather than relying purely on the audit firm's word.

Binance

Binance Proof of Reserves binance.com — proof of reserves →

What: Publishes monthly reserve-ratio attestations for major assets, with published PoR code available for public review. Independent commentators have noted these audits are point-in-time snapshots rather than continuous verification — worth keeping in mind when reading any single attestation.

Coinbase

Coinbase — SEC Filings investor.coinbase.com →

What: Coinbase does not run a crypto-native "proof of reserves" program. As a publicly listed company, it instead discloses assets and liabilities through SEC-filed, Deloitte-audited financial statements under PCAOB standards — a different verification model, not an absence of one.

These firms audit smart contracts and protocols for security vulnerabilities — relevant primarily to developers and project teams deciding whether to trust a contract, not to individual consumers directly. If you're an individual, an audit report from one of these firms (published by the project itself) is a signal worth checking for, not a tool you'd use yourself.

Hacken

Section 8 · Scam Reporting Authorities

Where to actually report a crypto scam or fraud, by region — distinct from Section 1's licensing regulators. Use our free Crypto Scam Red Flags Checklist to confirm what you're dealing with, and our Crypto Complaint Generator to draft the actual report.

US — FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)

Federal Bureau of Investigation ic3.gov →

What: The FBI's official portal for reporting internet-facilitated crime, including crypto investment fraud and pig-butchering scams.

US — FTC ReportFraud

Federal Trade Commission reportfraud.ftc.gov →

What: The FTC's consumer fraud reporting portal, feeding its Consumer Sentinel Network used across federal and state law enforcement.

UK — Action Fraud

Action Fraud actionfraud.police.uk →

What: The UK's national reporting centre for fraud and cybercrime, including crypto investment scams.

EU — Europol

Europol europol.europa.eu →

What: EU law enforcement coordination — for reporting, use your own country's national police or cybercrime unit; Europol supports cross-border investigation rather than taking individual reports directly.

Australia — ReportCyber (ACSC)

Australian Cyber Security Centre cyber.gov.au/report →

What: Australia's cybercrime reporting portal, for crypto scams involving hacking, account compromise, or online fraud.

Australia — Scamwatch (ACCC)

Australian Competition and Consumer Commission scamwatch.gov.au →

What: Australia's consumer scam-reporting and awareness portal — for scams specifically, distinct from ASIC's regulatory/licensing role in Section 1.
