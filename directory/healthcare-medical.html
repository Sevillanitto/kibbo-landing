Healthcare & Medical

Regulators, professional verification, insurance and billing bodies, records/privacy authorities, medicine safety, and complaint routes — for the UK, US, EU, and Australia.

← All free resources

Healthcare complaints span several genuinely different bodies depending on what actually went wrong — a regulator that sets and enforces standards is not the same as an insurance ombudsman, which is not the same as the route for complaining about the care you personally received. This directory keeps those apart deliberately: the same organization sometimes appears in more than one section below because it serves a different purpose each time, not because of a duplicate.

Need to write to a provider, insurer, or pharmacy? Use our free letter generators to draft a complaint, records request, or billing dispute letter.

Section 1 · Healthcare Regulators & Consumer Authorities

Who sets and enforces healthcare standards — the general oversight bodies, not a specific insurance, records, or complaints function (those are Sections 3, 4, and 6). The EU has no single equivalent: healthcare regulation is a member-state competence, not an EU-wide one, so no single body is listed here for the EU.

Health and social care regulator (England)

Care Quality Commission (CQC) cqc.org.uk →

What: The independent regulator of health and social care organizations in England — hospitals, care homes, GP practices, dentists, and more. Inspects and rates providers, and can take enforcement action.

When to use: To check a provider's inspection rating before choosing them, or to understand who actually regulates a specific type of care setting.

Doctor regulator (UK-wide)

General Medical Council (GMC) gmc-uk.org →

What: The statutory regulator for individual doctors practising in the UK — sets standards, maintains the register, and can restrict or remove a doctor's ability to practise.

When to use: To understand the standards a doctor is held to, separate from checking their specific registration (Section 2) or raising a concern about one (Section 6).

Patient rights and HIPAA oversight (US)

HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) hhs.gov/ocr →

What: The federal office enforcing HIPAA (health information privacy), conscience/religious freedom protections, and civil rights law in healthcare. Also the body with jurisdiction over medical records privacy specifically (see Section 4).

When to use: As the general federal oversight body for patient rights and health information privacy in the US.

Medicare/Medicaid regulator and hospital oversight (US)

Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) cms.gov →

What: The federal agency administering Medicare, Medicaid, and setting conditions of participation that hospitals and other providers must meet to receive federal funding. Also the source of hospital price transparency rules (see Section 7).

When to use: As a starting point for Medicare/Medicaid-related issues or questions about federal hospital oversight standards.

National health practitioner regulator (Australia)

Australian Health Practitioner Regulation Agency (AHPRA) ahpra.gov.au →

What: Australia's single national scheme regulating doctors, dentists, physiotherapists, pharmacists, and 15 professions in total under one register and one set of National Boards — unlike the UK, which regulates each profession through a separate body (Section 2).

When to use: As the starting point for any Australian health practitioner regulation question, before checking a specific register (Section 2) or lodging a notification (Section 6).

National safety and quality standards body (Australia)

Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care safetyandquality.gov.au →

What: The national statutory authority that develops Australia's health service safety and quality standards, and publishes the Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights (see Section 9).

When to use: To understand the national standards Australian health services are held to.

Section 2 · Medical Provider & Professional Verification

Check whether a specific individual practitioner — doctor, dentist, optician, physiotherapist, or other health professional — is genuinely registered and in good standing. In the UK, each profession is regulated separately; in Australia, one national register covers all of them.

Check a doctor's registration (UK)

GMC — List of Registered Medical Practitioners gmc-uk.org →

What: Search the public register to confirm a doctor is licensed to practise in the UK, and check for any restrictions on their registration.

When to use: Before or during treatment, to independently verify a doctor's registration status.

Check a dentist's registration (UK)

General Dental Council (GDC) gdc-uk.org →

What: The UK regulator for dentists and dental care professionals — maintains the register and sets standards for the dental profession specifically.

When to use: Before private or NHS dental treatment, to confirm the practitioner is registered.

Check a nurse or midwife's registration (UK)

Nursing and Midwifery Council (NMC) nmc.org.uk →

What: The UK regulator for nurses, midwives, and nursing associates.

When to use: To verify a nurse or midwife's registration status.

Check a physiotherapist or allied health professional's registration (UK)

Health and Care Professions Council (HCPC) hcpc-uk.org →

What: Regulates 15 allied health professions in the UK, including physiotherapists, paramedics, radiographers, and dietitians.

When to use: To verify registration for a physiotherapist or other allied health professional specifically — not covered by the GMC, GDC, or NMC.

Check an optician's registration (UK)

General Optical Council (GOC) optical.org →

What: The UK regulator for optometrists, dispensing opticians, and optical businesses.

When to use: Before an eye test or optical treatment, to confirm the practitioner or business is registered.

Find your state medical board (US)

Federation of State Medical Boards (FSMB) fsmb.org →

What: Physician licensing and verification in the US is handled by each state's own medical board — there is no single national register. FSMB is the umbrella organization for all state boards and the narrowest reliable federal-level starting point, kept deliberately narrow rather than listing all 50+ individual boards here.

When to use: To find and be directed to your specific state's medical board for license verification or disciplinary history.

Check any registered practitioner (Australia — one register, 15 professions)

AHPRA — Register of Practitioners ahpra.gov.au/Registration/Registers-of-Practitioners →

What: A single national register covering doctors, dentists, physiotherapists, pharmacists, optometrists, and every other AHPRA-regulated profession — unlike the UK, there's no need to check a separate register per profession.

When to use: Before or during any treatment in Australia, regardless of which of the 15 regulated professions is involved.

Section 3 · Health Insurance & Claims

Insurance-specific dispute bodies only — for a denied or disputed health insurance claim. Not the general regulator (Section 1) or the care-complaint route (Section 6). No single EU-wide insurance ombudsman exists; insurance dispute resolution is handled at member-state level.

Escalate a health insurance dispute (UK)

Financial Ombudsman Service financial-ombudsman.org.uk →

What: The UK's free, independent ombudsman for financial services complaints, including private health insurance disputes — a broader remit than health specifically, but the correct route for an insurance claim dispute.

When to use: After your insurer's own complaints process hasn't resolved a disputed or denied claim.

Find your state insurance regulator (US)

National Association of Insurance Commissioners (NAIC) naic.org →

What: Health insurance regulation in the US is handled by each state's own insurance commissioner — there is no single national regulator. NAIC is the umbrella organization and narrowest reliable federal-level starting point, kept narrow rather than listing all 50+ state commissioners here.

When to use: To find and be directed to your specific state's insurance commissioner for a health insurance complaint.

Dispute a private health insurance decision (Australia)

Australian Financial Complaints Authority (AFCA) afca.org.au →

What: The free, independent external dispute resolution body for private health insurance complaints in Australia, once your insurer's own internal process is exhausted.

When to use: After lodging an internal complaint with your health fund and not reaching a resolution.

Section 4 · Medical Records, Privacy & Data

Who to contact for a medical records access issue or a health data privacy complaint specifically.

Report a health data privacy violation (UK)

Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) ico.org.uk →

What: The UK's independent data protection regulator, covering health records and medical data under UK GDPR.

When to use: If a healthcare provider mishandled your medical records or health data, after raising it with the provider directly.

Report a medical records access or HIPAA violation (US)

HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) hhs.gov/ocr →

What: The federal body enforcing HIPAA's right of access — your legal right to obtain a copy of your own medical records, generally within 30 days of a request.

When to use: If a provider denies or unreasonably delays a records request, or mishandles your health information.

Complain about health data mishandling (Australia)

Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) oaic.gov.au →

What: Australia's privacy regulator, covering health records and My Health Record complaints under the Privacy Act 1988.

When to use: If a healthcare provider or My Health Record mishandled your health information.

Understand your EU health data rights

European Data Protection Board (EDPB) edpb.europa.eu →

What: The EU body coordinating data protection authorities across member states and issuing guidance on GDPR, including health data (a special category under Article 9). Complaints themselves are still filed with your own national Data Protection Authority, not the EDPB directly.

When to use: To understand your EU-wide health data rights before filing a complaint with your specific national DPA.

Section 5 · Medicines, Medical Devices & Product Safety

The national authority that approves and monitors the safety of medicines and medical devices. These same bodies may also appear in Section 8 for the pharmacy-dispensing angle specifically — that's intentional, not a duplicate.

Medicines and device safety regulator (UK)

Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) gov.uk — MHRA →

What: The UK regulator for the safety and efficacy of medicines and medical devices.

When to use: To check safety information about a medicine or device, or report a serious side effect or fault.

Medicines and device safety regulator (US)

Food and Drug Administration (FDA) fda.gov →

What: The US regulator responsible for approving and monitoring the safety of drugs and medical devices.

When to use: To check a medicine's approval status, or report a serious adverse event or device malfunction.

Medicines and device safety regulator (Australia)

Therapeutic Goods Administration (TGA) tga.gov.au →

What: Australia's regulator for the safety and efficacy of medicines and medical devices, maintaining the Australian Register of Therapeutic Goods (ARTG).

When to use: To check whether a product is legally registered for sale in Australia, or report a safety concern.

Medicines safety regulator (EU)

European Medicines Agency (EMA) ema.europa.eu →

What: The EU-wide agency responsible for the scientific evaluation and safety monitoring of medicines authorised for use across the EU. Medical device regulation, by contrast, is coordinated at EU level but enforced by each member state's own competent authority.

When to use: To check EU-wide safety information about a medicine authorised centrally.

Section 6 · Healthcare Complaints & Disputes

How to formally complain about the care you personally received — distinct from the general regulator (Section 1) and from an insurance dispute (Section 3).

Report a care quality concern (England) — see Section 1

See Section 1 · Care Quality Commission (CQC) Jump to Section 1 →

What: The CQC (already listed in Section 1 as the general regulator) is also the route for reporting a care quality concern about a specific provider — cross-referenced here rather than duplicating the full entry.

When to use: To report a serious concern about a specific care provider's standards, as distinct from a complaint about an individual doctor (see the GMC entry below).

Raise a concern about a doctor (UK)

GMC — Raise a Concern gmc-uk.org/concerns/raise-a-concern →

What: The GMC's process for patients to raise a concern about a doctor's behaviour, health, or performance. Free, with an online form and a support service for complainants.

When to use: For a serious concern about an individual doctor specifically, not a general care-quality issue at a facility (see CQC above).

Lodge a notification about a practitioner (Australia)

AHPRA — Notifications ahpra.gov.au/Notifications →

What: AHPRA's formal complaints process (called a "notification") about a registered practitioner's health, conduct, or performance — reviewed by the relevant National Board, which decides whether regulatory action is needed.

When to use: To raise a concern about any AHPRA-registered practitioner, across all 15 regulated professions.

Find where to file a complaint about a doctor (US)

DocInfo (Federation of State Medical Boards) docinfo.org/report-a-doctor →

What: A consumer-facing site run by the Federation of State Medical Boards that explains when and how to file a complaint against a doctor, and directs you to your specific state medical board — complaints themselves are handled state-by-state, not federally.

When to use: As the starting point to find your specific state's complaint process for a doctor.

Section 7 · Healthcare Costs, Pricing & Billing

Understanding what you should actually be charged, and where to go for a billing-specific issue.

Look up a hospital's actual prices (US)

CMS — Hospital Price Transparency cms.gov — Hospital Price Transparency →

What: Under federal rules, US hospitals must publicly post real, standard charges and payer-specific negotiated rates in a machine-readable format. This page explains the requirement and links to enforcement and compliance resources.

When to use: Before a planned procedure, to check what a hospital is required to disclose about pricing.

Medical debt and billing complaint resources (US)

Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB) consumerfinance.gov →

What: The CFPB regulates how medical debt can be reported to credit bureaus and collected, and takes complaints about medical debt collection and billing-related credit reporting issues.

When to use: If a medical bill was sent to collections unfairly, or reported to your credit file incorrectly.

Check what you should actually be charged (UK)

NHS — Help with Health Costs nhs.uk — Help with Health Costs →

What: Official NHS guidance on which NHS services carry a charge (prescriptions, dental, optical), current charge amounts, and who qualifies for an exemption or reduced-cost help scheme.

When to use: Before assuming an NHS charge is correct, or to check if you qualify for an exemption.

What: Official guidance on Medicare benefit amounts, the Medicare Safety Net, and what you're likely to be out of pocket for out-of-hospital and hospital services.

Section 8 · Pharmacies & Medicines

The dispensing-pharmacy angle specifically — regulators of pharmacies and pharmacists, and how to verify a medicine or online pharmacy is genuine. MHRA, FDA, and TGA also appear in Section 5 for their broader medicine/device safety role — the listing here is deliberately about the pharmacy-dispensing and counterfeit-verification angle instead.

Pharmacy and pharmacist regulator (UK)

General Pharmaceutical Council (GPhC) pharmacyregulation.org →

What: Regulates pharmacists, pharmacy technicians, and registered pharmacy premises in Great Britain.

When to use: To verify a pharmacy or pharmacist is genuinely registered.

Report a fake or counterfeit medicine (UK)

MHRA — Yellow Card Scheme gov.uk — Yellow Card Scheme →

What: The Yellow Card scheme is MHRA's system for reporting suspected side effects, defective medical products, and suspected counterfeit/falsified medicines and devices.

When to use: If you suspect a medicine you bought — especially online — is fake or counterfeit.

Find your state pharmacy board (US)

National Association of Boards of Pharmacy (NABP) nabp.pharmacy →

What: Pharmacy regulation in the US is handled state-by-state — there is no single national pharmacy board. NABP is the umbrella organization and narrowest reliable federal-level starting point, kept narrow rather than listing all 50+ state boards.

When to use: To find and be directed to your specific state's pharmacy board.

Verify an online pharmacy is legitimate (US)

FDA — BeSafeRx fda.gov — BeSafeRx →

What: The FDA's official campaign and resource for verifying whether an online pharmacy is state-licensed and legitimate, and recognizing the warning signs of a fraudulent one.

When to use: Before buying prescription medicine from an online pharmacy you haven't used before.

Pharmacist regulator (Australia)

Pharmacy Board of Australia pharmacyboard.gov.au →

What: One of the 15 National Boards operating under AHPRA (see Section 1/2), responsible specifically for pharmacist registration standards and guidelines.

When to use: For pharmacy-specific regulatory guidance, or to verify a pharmacist via the main AHPRA register (Section 2).

Report a suspected fake or counterfeit medicine (Australia)

TGA — Counterfeit (Fake) Medicines and Medical Devices tga.gov.au — Counterfeit Medicines →

What: TGA guidance on spotting and reporting counterfeit medicines and medical devices, including online purchases.

When to use: If you suspect a medicine or device bought in Australia, especially online, is fake.

Verify a legal online pharmacy (EU)

European Commission — EU Common Logo for Online Pharmacies health.ec.europa.eu — EU Logo →

What: Since 2015, every online pharmacy or retailer legally selling medicines in the EU (plus Norway, Iceland, Liechtenstein) must display a common EU logo. Clicking the logo verifies the seller against the official national register — a genuine, independently confirmed verification mechanism, not a general information page.

When to use: Before buying medicine from an online pharmacy based in the EU — look for and click the logo to verify it's real.

Section 9 · Healthcare Laws & Official Guidance

The core patient-rights laws and official guidance documents underlying the sections above.

HIPAA Privacy Rule guidance (US)

HHS — HIPAA hhs.gov/hipaa →

What: Official HHS guidance explaining the HIPAA Privacy Rule — what it covers, your right of access, and how it protects health information.

When to use: To understand your actual rights under HIPAA before filing a complaint (Section 1/4).

NHS Constitution for England

GOV.UK — The NHS Constitution for England gov.uk — NHS Constitution →

What: Sets out the legal rights and pledges patients, the public, and staff are entitled to from the NHS in England — including the right to access NHS services, to be treated with dignity, and to complain.

When to use: To understand your baseline rights as an NHS patient before raising a complaint.

Australian Charter of Healthcare Rights

Australian Commission on Safety and Quality in Health Care safetyandquality.gov.au — Charter of Healthcare Rights →

What: Describes 7 rights everyone can expect when receiving healthcare in Australia, applying in every setting where care is provided.

When to use: As the baseline reference for what you're entitled to expect from any Australian healthcare provider.

GDPR — health data provisions (EU)

EUR-Lex — General Data Protection Regulation eur-lex.europa.eu — GDPR full text →

What: Health data is a "special category" under GDPR Article 9, subject to stricter processing conditions than ordinary personal data. This is the full legal text; see Section 4 for the practical complaints route via the EDPB and national DPAs.

When to use: For the underlying legal basis behind an EU health-data complaint or question.
