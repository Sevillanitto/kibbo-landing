Delivery & Parcels

Direct complaint links for every major carrier. No hold music.

← All free resources

Carriers bury their complaint forms behind customer service chatbots and FAQ pages. These are the direct links — plus the legal reminder that if you bought from a retailer, the retailer is legally responsible for delivery, not the carrier.

Once you know who's responsible, our free complaint letter generator can draft your claim in minutes.

Important: If you ordered from an online retailer, your contract is with the retailer — not the carrier. The retailer is legally responsible for delivery under the Consumer Rights Act 2015. Always claim from the retailer first. They must resolve it regardless of what the carrier says.

Section 2 · Postal Ombudsman Directory

Where to escalate once the carrier's own complaints process hasn't resolved it — a genuinely independent ombudsman, distinct from the carrier's own customer service.

UK — Postal Redress Service (POSTRS)

Postal Redress Service (POSTRS), managed by CEDR cedr-assist.com — POSTRS complaint →

What: Ofcom-approved independent dispute resolution for UK postal complaints, covering most major couriers (Royal Mail, Evri, DPD, and others). Free to use, and issues a decision binding on the carrier.

When to use: Only after the carrier's own complaints process is exhausted (a deadlock letter, or 8 weeks with no resolution). This only covers disputes where you have a direct contract with the carrier — a card chargeback or Section 75 claim against the retailer (see Section 6 below) is a separate route POSTRS does not handle.

US — no genuine individual-complaint postal ombudsman exists

Postal Regulatory Commission prc.gov — consumer assistance →

What: The Postal Regulatory Commission oversees USPS policy and rates broadly, but does not independently investigate or resolve individual consumer complaints — an inquiry submitted here is simply logged and forwarded to USPS itself for a response, not adjudicated by an independent body.

When to use: There is no US equivalent to the UK's POSTRS or Australia's Postal Industry Ombudsman for individual disputes — USPS's own claims process (Section 1 above) is the actual endpoint for a US postal complaint.

Australia — Postal Industry Ombudsman

Commonwealth Ombudsman — Postal Industry Ombudsman ombudsman.gov.au — postal industry complaints →

What: The Commonwealth Ombudsman, acting as the Postal Industry Ombudsman, genuinely investigates individual complaints about Australia Post and StarTrack — confirmed via its own published complaint-handling statistics.

When to use: Only after raising the complaint directly with Australia Post or StarTrack first (Section 1 above) — the Ombudsman requires this as a precondition.

Section 3 · Consumer Authorities

The general consumer protection regulator in each region — broader than parcel disputes specifically, but the right body once a delivery dispute becomes a wider consumer-rights issue.

US — Federal Trade Commission

U.S. Federal Trade Commission ftc.gov →

What: The US federal consumer protection regulator — the general complaint route for a retailer's or carrier's unfair or deceptive practices.

Australia — ACCC

Australian Competition & Consumer Commission accc.gov.au →

What: Australia's general consumer protection regulator — separate from the Postal Industry Ombudsman above (Section 2), which handles postal-specific service complaints specifically.

UK — Citizens Advice

Citizens Advice citizensadvice.org.uk →

What: The UK's general consumer advice and complaint-referral service — also the entry point to Trading Standards (below), since Trading Standards itself has no single national complaint portal.

EU — European Consumer Centres Network (ECC-Net)

ECC-Net eccnet.eu →

What: Routes cross-border EU consumer complaints — including delivery disputes — to the right national body, via 29 centres across the EU, Norway, and Iceland.

UK — Trading Standards (via Citizens Advice)

Citizens Advice — report to Trading Standards citizensadvice.org.uk — report to Trading Standards →

What: Trading Standards is administered locally with no single national complaint portal — Citizens Advice's consumer service is the actual entry point, logging the complaint and passing it to the relevant local Trading Standards authority.

Section 5 · Customs Authorities Directory

The official body governing customs duty, import VAT, and tariffs in each region — the actual authority behind a disputed import charge.

US — Customs and Border Protection

U.S. Customs and Border Protection cbp.gov →

What: The US federal agency governing customs duties, import regulations, and border enforcement.

UK — Tax and customs for goods sent from abroad

HM Revenue & Customs (via GOV.UK) gov.uk — goods sent from abroad →

What: The current official guidance on VAT, customs duty, and excise duty owed on goods sent to the UK from abroad, and how the carrier collects it on HMRC's behalf.

EU — Taxation and Customs Union

European Commission — Taxation and Customs Union taxation-customs.ec.europa.eu — customs →

What: The European Commission's customs policy hub. Actual customs administration is run by each member state's own national customs authority under EU-wide rules — this is the entry point, not a single national authority.

Australia — Australian Border Force

Australian Border Force abf.gov.au →

What: Australia's customs and border enforcement agency, within the Department of Home Affairs portfolio — governs import duties, GST on imports, and customs clearance.

Section 6 · Payment Dispute Resources

The specific dispute process runs through your own card issuer or bank — but the underlying rules and eligible reasons for a dispute are set at the network level, which is why these network-level pages are the stable reference point instead of individual bank pages.

Visa — Zero Liability & dispute rights

Visa visa.com — zero liability policy →

What: Visa's network-level cardholder protection policy against unauthorized transactions. The actual dispute is filed with your card-issuing bank, not Visa directly — this page explains the underlying network rule your bank applies.

Mastercard — Zero Liability protection

Mastercard mastercard.com — zero liability protection →

What: Mastercard's network-level cardholder protection policy against unauthorized transactions. As with Visa, the actual dispute is filed with your card-issuing bank.

American Express — dispute a charge

American Express americanexpress.com — dispute a charge →

What: Unlike Visa and Mastercard, American Express is typically both the network and the issuer, so this page doubles as the actual dispute-filing guidance, not just a network policy reference.

PayPal — Resolution Center

PayPal paypal.com — what is the Resolution Center →

What: PayPal isn't a card network — it's its own payment processor with its own dispute system, the Resolution Center. Access requires logging into your own account; this page explains what it covers and how disputes work.
