Buying from a third-party seller on Amazon in Europe, only to receive something defective or nothing like the listing, doesn't mean you're stuck with that seller's own restrictive return policy. However strict an independent seller's terms are, EU law overrides them.

## Your 14-day right applies regardless of the seller's own policy

The EU Consumer Rights Directive gives you an unconditional 14-day right of withdrawal for any online purchase — you can return an item simply because you changed your mind, and you're entitled to a full refund of the item price plus the standard initial delivery cost.

## Beyond 14 days: the 2-year legal guarantee

If a product develops a fault or breaks within the first two years, the EU's legal guarantee of conformity requires the seller to repair or replace it free of charge — this obligation sits with the seller, not Amazon itself, though Amazon's own systems are built to help enforce it.

## The A-to-z Guarantee — Amazon's own safety net

If a third-party seller stalls with automated responses or otherwise fails to honour these rights, Amazon's internal A-to-z Guarantee is designed to process automatic refunds where it's shown the seller breached the EU consumer framework. This is Amazon's own commercial protection layered on top of your legal rights, not a substitute for them.

## Exercising your withdrawal or defect claim

Start the request through the returns panel, clearly indicating withdrawal within your 14 days, or selecting "defective item" where the legal guarantee applies. Always keep your return shipping receipt with tracking.

## If Amazon blocks your account or wrongly denies a refund

If your account is suspended or a legitimate refund is denied citing internal usage policy, you have real options beyond Amazon's own ecosystem. Note that the EU's old Online Dispute Resolution (ODR) platform was discontinued in July 2025 and no longer accepts new complaints — don't rely on outdated guides pointing you there. Instead, contact your national European Consumer Centre (ECC-Net), which can intervene directly and free of charge, especially for cross-border purchases, or check the European Commission's current directory of national ADR bodies for a scheme covering your specific dispute type.
