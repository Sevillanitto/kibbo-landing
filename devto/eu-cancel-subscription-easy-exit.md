Signing up for a subscription with one tap on your phone, only to discover cancelling requires an international premium-rate phone call or a signed physical letter, feels like an obvious injustice — and increasingly, it's one the law is catching up to. But it's important to be precise about which cancellation right you're actually using, because two very different rules apply depending on your timing.

## Rule 1: within your first 14 days — this IS EU-wide law, right now

If you're still within the 14-day right-of-withdrawal window from signing up, Directive (EU) 2023/2673 requires online sellers across the entire EU to provide a dedicated withdrawal function directly on their site or app — the "withdrawal button" covered in our guide to the 14-day right of withdrawal. This has applied EU-wide since 19 June 2026. Within this window, cancelling genuinely must be as easy as signing up was, by law, in every member state.

## Rule 2: cancelling an ongoing subscription after that window — this is NOT yet uniform EU law

This is where a lot of guides overstate what's currently guaranteed. Once you're past the initial 14 days and want to cancel an ongoing subscription, there is no single EU-wide "cancellation button" law yet. What exists instead is a patchwork of strong national laws in specific member states: Germany's "Kündigungsbutton" (in force since 2022, requiring a clearly visible, permanently accessible cancellation button with a simple confirmation step, and no forced redirect to phone or chat support); France has a comparable obligation under its Consumer Code. Other member states have less developed or no equivalent requirement yet. A genuinely EU-wide version of this rule — an "easy cancellation facility" modelled closely on the German and French approach — is under active development as part of the proposed Digital Fairness Act, but that has not been adopted yet. Don't assume your specific country guarantees a one-click cancellation button for an ongoing subscription until you've checked its national law.

## Exercising your withdrawal right, if you're still in the 14-day window

Use the seller's dedicated withdrawal function if it's live, or send the EU's standard model withdrawal form. You're entitled to a pro-rata refund for any unused portion of the subscription period.

## Cancelling an older, ongoing subscription

Where your country has a cancellation-button law (Germany and France currently being the clearest examples), use it and request written confirmation — an "End of Contract Notice" — as proof the cancellation was actually processed. Where no such button exists yet in your country, check your contract's own cancellation terms and your national consumer protection agency for what's required.

## If the business ignores your cancellation and keeps charging you

If a business continues billing you after you've validly cancelled through its own channel, you have a separate and powerful lever regardless of which country you're in: contact your bank. Under the Payment Services Directive, you have the right to revoke a direct debit mandate or a recurring card payment token immediately and free of charge, blocking any future charge from that specific merchant.
