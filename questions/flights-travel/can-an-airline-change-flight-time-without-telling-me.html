Can an airline change my flight time without telling me in advance?

They can move the schedule — but move it far enough, and you get to decide whether you're still going.

Kibbo Consumer Desk·Updated August 2026·6 min read
Short answer

Airlines change schedules constantly, and they're generally expected to notify you when they do. The real question isn't whether they can change it — it's whether the change is big enough to count as "significant." If it is (a large time shift, a different airport, an added connection, a cabin downgrade), both the US and UK give you the right to reject the new itinerary and get your money back instead of just accepting whatever they've rebooked you onto.

What the law says

United StatesUnited Kingdom
What counts as "significant"Departure/arrival moved 3+ hours (domestic) or 6+ hours (international); different airport; added connection; cabin downgradeBroadly similar concept — a large enough earlier departure can be treated as a cancellation

Your right if it's minor (e.g. a 20-30 min shift)No refund right — the airline isn't required to do anything beyond notifying youGenerally no compensation right for small shifts
Flight brought forward within 14 days of departureNot addressed the same specific wayCan be treated as a cancellation — refund and compensation may apply

The distinction between a flight moved earlier versus later matters more than people expect. A delay is inconvenient, but you can usually still make your connections and plans work around it. A flight moved earlier can cause you to miss it entirely through no fault of your own — which is exactly why UK/EU rules treat an early-moved flight within 14 days of departure the same as an outright cancellation, with both refund/re-routing rights and possible compensation, not just a courtesy notification.

One more nuance: US airlines occasionally re-number a flight — same route, same time, new flight number — without any real change to your journey. Under the DOT's current enforcement pause (through mid-2027 while the definition is redrafted), this narrow scenario alone, with genuinely nothing else changed, doesn't currently trigger the automatic refund right. But if the airport, connection count, cabin, or timing changed at all alongside the renumbering, that's a real change and the refund right still applies.

Your situation — which one is this?

Significant — you can decline Your flight was moved 4+ hours earlier or later, or to a different airport

This clears the threshold in both the US and UK. You have the right to reject the revised itinerary and receive a refund to your original payment method, rather than accept whatever the airline rebooked you onto.

Significant, UK/EU specifically Your flight was moved earlier within 14 days of departure

Treat this as a cancellation, not a mere schedule change. You may be entitled to compensation in addition to your refund or re-routing rights — this is a commonly missed entitlement because the airline's notification often just says "schedule change," not "cancellation."

Not significant on its own A 20-30 minute shift with no other changes

Small shifts like this generally don't trigger a refund right on their own in either jurisdiction. The airline typically just needs to notify you.

Watch for this A "schedule change" notification that's actually a cancellation in disguise

Some notifications are worded neutrally ("your flight time has been adjusted") when the underlying change is large enough to legally qualify as a cancellation or significant change. Compare the original confirmation to the new one directly — don't rely on how the airline chooses to label the email.

Where airlines get it wrong

Legitimate

"Your flight has been moved from 2:00pm to 9:00pm. This qualifies as a significant change under our policy — you're welcome to accept the new time, or decline and receive a full refund."

Overreach

Sending a generic "schedule update" notice for a change that's actually large enough to qualify as significant, without mentioning the refund/decline option at all — leaving the passenger to assume the new time is simply mandatory.

What to say

You, declining the revised itinerary "I've been notified of a schedule change to flight [number] that I consider significant [state why: X-hour shift / different airport / added connection]. I'm declining the revised itinerary and requesting my refund to my original form of payment."
You, if the airline disputes it's "significant" "Comparing my original confirmation to the revised one: [describe the specific difference]. This meets the threshold for a significant change under [DOT rules / UK261], so I'd like my refund processed on that basis."

If the airline disputes it

  1. Keep both confirmations — original and revised — side by side as your evidence.
  2. Point to the specific threshold that makes it qualify (hours, airport change, added connection, cabin downgrade).
  3. Escalate to DOT (US) or the CAA/an approved ADR body (UK) if the airline refuses the refund despite the change clearly meeting the threshold.
