Can an airline bump me from a flight even after I've checked in?

Checking in doesn't protect you from an oversold flight — but if you're bumped involuntarily, the airline owes you real cash, not just an apology and a later flight.

Kibbo Consumer Desk · Updated August 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

Yes — checking in doesn't guarantee your seat if the flight is oversold. But there's a hard legal line between being voluntarily bumped (you agreed to a deal) and involuntarily bumped (they forced you off). If it's involuntary and you did everything right — confirmed booking, checked in on time, met the boarding deadline — both the US and UK require the airline to pay you cash compensation on top of getting you to your destination. This is one of the few areas of air travel where the money is close to guaranteed, and yet most bumped passengers never claim it because they don't realize the compensation is owed automatically, not something you have to fight for in court.

What the law says

Overbooking itself is completely legal. Airlines are allowed to sell more seats than a plane has, betting that some passengers won't show up. The law doesn't step in to stop that practice — it steps in at the moment they get the bet wrong and have to remove someone who's already checked in.

United States United Kingdom
Legal basis 14 CFR Part 250 (DOT oversales rule) UK261, Articles 4 & 7
Must ask for volunteers first Yes, always, before involuntarily bumping anyone Yes, same requirement
Compensation — short delay 200% of one-way fare, capped at $1,075, if rebooked to arrive 1–2h late domestic (1–4h international) £220 (≈€250) for flights up to 1,500 km
Compensation — longer delay 400% of one-way fare, capped at $2,150, if later than the above £350–£520 (≈€400–€600) for longer routes
When nothing is owed Rebooked within 1 hour of original arrival, or you missed the carrier's stated check-in deadline Rerouted with only a short arrival delay (compensation may be halved on some long-haul cases)
How it must be paid Same day, cash or check — a voucher only if you choose it Immediately at the airport, cash or bank transfer

Two things worth flagging because they trip people up. First, in the US these caps were only raised in January 2025 — from $775/$1,550 to $1,075/$2,150 — and by regulation they're reviewed for inflation every two years, so it's worth a quick check that the figure hasn't moved again since. Second, in the UK a wider reform agreed in 2025 is closing a loophole airlines have used for years: the so-called "no-show" clause, which let a carrier deny you your return or onward flight simply because you didn't use an earlier leg of the same ticket. Once that change lands, being blocked from a later leg this way will itself count as denied boarding — with compensation owed. UK261 hasn't formally adopted it yet, but it shows the direction the rule is moving, and airlines that already apply the old practice are on increasingly thin ice.

Your situation — which one is this?

"I got bumped" covers a few genuinely different situations, and the compensation picture changes depending on which one you're actually in.

Covered You checked in on time, they still turned you away at the gate

This is textbook involuntary denied boarding. You did nothing wrong — the airline oversold the flight and ran out of seats. You're entitled to full compensation under whichever jurisdiction's rules apply, on top of a refund or rebooking.

Not the same thing A gate agent asked for volunteers and you said yes

If you accepted a deal — a travel voucher, a later flight, lounge access, whatever was offered — that's a voluntary bump, and it's a private negotiation, not a statutory entitlement. The airline can offer whatever it wants, and you can accept, counter, or refuse. If you refuse and they force you off anyway, that turns it involuntary again — see above.

Watch for this You're pressured to "volunteer" without being told the involuntary amount first

A known pattern at busy gates: an agent offers a $200 voucher for volunteers, without mentioning that involuntary compensation could be worth $1,075–$2,150 in cash. Nothing requires the airline to disclose that number to you before you agree to volunteer — so before accepting anything, ask directly: "What would I be owed if you bumped me involuntarily instead?" That question alone often improves the voluntary offer considerably.

Reduced or no compensation Missed check-in deadline, invalid documents, or a downgrade you accepted

If the airline can show you didn't meet its stated check-in cutoff, or that your travel documents weren't in order, compensation typically doesn't apply — this is one of the narrow carve-outs both systems allow. Keep your boarding pass and check-in confirmation time as proof if this is disputed.

Where airlines get it wrong (and passengers let them)

Almost every dispute over a bump comes down to the airline treating an involuntary situation as if it were voluntary, or quietly hoping you don't know the difference.

Legitimate

"We're oversold on this flight. We're looking for volunteers first, and here's what we're offering. If we don't get enough, we'll have to deny boarding involuntarily to some passengers, and they'll be compensated according to the DOT/UK261 schedule."

Overreach

"There's nothing we can do, this happens sometimes, here's a $150 voucher and we'll get you on the next flight." — presented as if it's the only option, with no mention of a cash entitlement, and no attempt to ask for volunteers first.

Legitimate

Paying the statutory amount in cash or check on the spot, or by mail within 24 hours if you're rebooked onto a flight that departs before payment can be arranged.

Overreach

Offering only a travel credit and describing it as "the compensation" — a voucher is not the same as your legal entitlement, and you can insist on cash or check instead.

If a gate agent tells you compensation "isn't available today" or that it's "airline policy not to pay cash" — that's not a legal position, it's a hope that you'll accept it. The regulation doesn't have a carve-out for a busy day.

What to say, scenario by scenario

At the gate, being told you're bumped involuntarily

You, to the gate agent "I have a confirmed reservation and checked in within your deadline — I did not volunteer. I understand I'm entitled to compensation under [14 CFR 250.5 / UK261], payable today in cash or check, separate from my rebooking or refund. Please process that now, along with a written statement of the reason for the denial."

If they offer only a voucher

You, pushing back "I'd like the statutory compensation in cash or check, as I'm entitled to under the regulation — a travel voucher isn't the same thing, and I'm choosing not to accept one in place of it."

If they claim you don't qualify

You, asking for the reason in writing "Can you tell me specifically why you believe I don't qualify — was it the check-in deadline, documentation, or something else? I'd like that reason in writing, along with your name and the flight number, so I can follow up if needed."

If they still refuse

  1. Get it in writing at the airport. Before you leave the counter, get the agent's name, the stated reason for refusal, and the flight number and date — a phone photo of a written note is enough.
  2. File a complaint with the regulator. In the US, that's the DOT's Aviation Consumer Protection division. In the UK, escalate first to the airline's own complaints process (they have up to 8 weeks to respond), then to an approved Alternative Dispute Resolution (ADR) body or the CAA if unresolved.
  3. Keep everything. Boarding pass, original and revised itinerary, check-in confirmation time, and any written refusal. These are exactly what a regulator or ADR body will ask for.
  4. Don't accept a voucher under pressure at the gate if you'd rather have cash. Once you accept it, you've made your choice — there's no obligation to decide on the spot, and a rushed "sign here" moment at a crowded gate is precisely when this gets missed.
