Can an airline blame bad weather to avoid paying me compensation?

Real weather is a genuine excuse. A vague reference to "operational reasons" is not — and there's a specific legal test that separates the two.

Kibbo Consumer Desk·Updated August 2026·7 min read
Short answer

Only if the weather genuinely caused the disruption. Under UK261, real "extraordinary circumstances" — including severe weather — do let an airline off the hook for cash compensation. But the airline has to prove the disruption was unavoidable even with all reasonable measures taken, and courts have repeatedly narrowed what actually qualifies. Airlines are known to reach for "weather" or "operational reasons" as a catch-all excuse for problems that are really their own fault — a mechanical issue, a crew shortage, a late-arriving aircraft — none of which qualify as extraordinary, no matter how the airline phrases it.

What the law says

The legal test that matters (established through UK/EU case law) is whether the disruption was genuinely unavoidable "even if all reasonable measures had been taken." A vague reference to "operational reasons" or "weather" without any specifics doesn't meet that bar — the airline has to be able to say what happened, when, and why nothing could have been done about it.

Knock-on effects are a particularly tricky area. If your morning flight is cancelled because the aircraft assigned to it was delayed by yesterday's storm, the chain of causation has to stay tight — an airline can't indefinitely blame yesterday's weather for today's schedule chaos. The longer and more indirect the chain, the weaker the "extraordinary circumstances" claim becomes.

Your situation — which one is this?

Genuinely excused An airport-wide ground stop for a storm, or an ATC-ordered slot delay

If every airline at the airport was affected the same way, and the cause is documented (weather advisories, ATC notices), this is a strong case for a legitimate extraordinary circumstance. Compensation likely doesn't apply, though care (meals, hotel) still does.

Often over-claimed "Operational reasons" with no further detail

This phrase alone tells you nothing. It's frequently used to cover mechanical problems, crew scheduling issues, or a late-arriving aircraft — none of which are extraordinary circumstances. Ask for specifics before accepting the refusal.

Not excused A technical fault, even an unexpected one

Courts have settled this repeatedly: a routine technical fault, even one the airline didn't see coming, is considered part of the normal business of operating aircraft, not an extraordinary circumstance. This applies even to faults flagged by the manufacturer, unless it's a genuinely rare, hidden manufacturing defect.

Depends on the chain A knock-on delay from a previous day's disruption

If the airline is blaming a weather event from a day or more earlier for today's delay, the causal chain needs to be direct and tight. The more steps between the original weather event and your specific delay, the more that claim should be questioned.

Where airlines get it wrong

Legitimate

"Your flight was affected by an air traffic control ground stop issued at [airport] due to severe weather at [time]. This is documented as an ATC restriction and qualifies as an extraordinary circumstance — compensation doesn't apply, but you're still entitled to meals and accommodation."

Overreach

"The delay was due to operational reasons beyond our control" — with no date, no specific event, and no way to verify it, offered as a blanket reason to deny compensation across an entire flight's worth of passengers.

What to say

When first told it's an extraordinary circumstance

You, requesting specifics "You've cited extraordinary circumstances for flight [number]. Please specify in writing what the circumstance was, when it occurred, and why it could not have been avoided even with all reasonable measures taken. I'm requesting this before accepting the refusal of compensation."

If the explanation is vague

You, following up "Operational reasons is not a specific cause. Was this a mechanical issue, a crew matter, or a weather/ATC event? Each of these is treated differently under UK261, and I'd like to know which applies before I accept this decision."

If they won't specify

  1. Ask again, in writing, and keep a record of both your request and their response.
  2. Check public flight-tracking data (Flightradar24 or FlightAware) for actual conditions that day, and whether other airlines' flights at the same airport operated normally — if they did, that weakens a genuine weather claim considerably.
  3. Escalate to an approved ADR body or the CAA if the airline won't substantiate the claim with specifics.
