Who pays if someone uses my card without permission?

The number that decides how much this costs you isn't the fraud amount — it's how many hours pass before you report it.

Kibbo Consumer Desk · Updated September 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

In almost all cases, your bank or card provider bears the loss, not you — provided you report the unauthorized use promptly. Both the US and UK cap your maximum personal liability by law, and in most real-world situations you can end up paying nothing at all. The single biggest factor in how much you're on the hook for isn't the amount stolen — it's how quickly you report it once you notice.

What the law says

This is one of the more genuinely consumer-favorable areas of banking law on both sides of the Atlantic, but the exact numbers differ, and the US distinguishes between debit and credit cards in a way the UK doesn't.

United States United Kingdom
Legal basis — debit Regulation E, 12 CFR § 1005.6 Payment Services Regulations 2017, regs. 76-77
Legal basis — credit Regulation Z / Fair Credit Billing Act Same Payment Services Regulations 2017 cover both debit and credit
Maximum liability — debit, reported fast $50 if reported within 2 business days of discovering the loss Provider must refund by end of the next business day after you report it — effectively same-day resolution in most cases
Maximum liability — debit, reported late Up to $500 if reported within 60 days of your statement; potentially unlimited after 60 days No equivalent 60-day cliff — the Regulations still require prompt refund once reported, though delay can affect how the claim is assessed
Maximum liability — credit Capped at $50 by federal law regardless of timing; most issuers voluntarily offer $0 liability, especially for card-not-present (online) fraud Same £0-in-practice outcome in most cases, refunded by end of next business day
If the bank can show negligence The $50/$500/unlimited tiers above already account for this Up to £35 can be deducted from your refund if the provider proves you failed to protect your PIN or security details; refund can be refused entirely if they show you acted fraudulently

The US debit-card tiers are the sharpest cliff in this entire area of law: $50 if you report within 2 business days, up to $500 within 60 days, and potentially unlimited liability after that. That's a genuinely different outcome depending on whether you happen to check your statement weekly or let it pile up for two months — which is exactly why the advice everywhere is the same: report the moment you notice, don't wait to "look into it yourself" first.

Your situation — which one is this?

Best case, most common You noticed within a day or two and reported it immediately

This is the scenario the law is built around, and it's also the most common outcome for people who check their accounts regularly. Expect a fast refund and minimal or zero liability on both sides of the Atlantic.

Still covered, act now You noticed weeks later while reviewing a statement

You're likely still within the US 60-day window for debit cards (capped at $500) and still protected under the UK's Payment Services Regulations. Don't delay any further — report it today, since every additional day matters more the longer it's already been.

Watch for this Your provider claims you were negligent with your PIN or card

A provider deducting the UK's £35 or applying the US negligence-related tiers needs to actually demonstrate this — writing your PIN on the card, sharing it with someone, or similarly clear carelessness. A general claim of "you must have been careless somehow" without specific evidence isn't sufficient grounds, and you can push back on it.

Different situation You authorized the transaction but regret it, or a subscription you forgot about charged you

This isn't unauthorized use in the legal sense — the card was used with your consent at the time, even if you now wish you hadn't. These liability protections apply to fraud, not buyer's remorse; a forgotten subscription is a separate cancellation issue, not a fraud dispute.

Where this goes wrong

Handled well

You report the moment you notice, get written confirmation of the report date, and the provider refunds you within their statutory window without unnecessary friction.

Handled badly

The provider drags out the "investigation" for weeks with no interim update, or tries to shift blame onto you without specific evidence of negligence.

Handled well

You freeze or cancel the card immediately alongside the report, minimizing any further unauthorized activity while the claim is processed.

Handled badly

You report the fraud but keep using the same card number, allowing further unauthorized charges to happen while you wait.

What to say

Reporting the transaction

You, contacting your provider "I want to report an unauthorized transaction on my account dated [date] for [amount]. I did not make or authorize this charge. Please cancel/freeze the card and confirm in writing the date and time you received this report."

If they push back on the timeline

You, pushing back "Under [Regulation E / the Payment Services Regulations], my refund timeline is tied to the date I reported this — can you confirm that date is now on record, and when I should expect the refund?"

If they claim negligence

You, asking for specifics "Can you tell me specifically what evidence you have that I failed to protect my card or PIN? I'd like that in writing before any deduction is applied to my refund."

If they still refuse

  1. Report immediately — the moment you notice, not after you've tried to figure it out yourself.
  2. Freeze or cancel the card at the same time as reporting.
  3. Get written confirmation of the exact date and time your report was received.
  4. US: escalate to the CFPB at consumerfinance.gov/complaint if your provider denies a claim that should be covered.
  5. UK: escalate to the Financial Ombudsman Service if unresolved after 8 weeks, or if you disagree with the provider's final response.
