Can a bank refuse to give me cash from my own account?

It's your money — but "your money" and "instant, no-questions-asked access to it" turn out to be two different legal ideas.

Kibbo Consumer Desk · Updated September 2026 · 7 min read
Short answer

Yes. Banks can legally delay or refuse a cash withdrawal — even from your own account — if they have reasonable grounds tied to fraud prevention, anti-money-laundering rules, or their own account terms and conditions. There's no US or UK law that guarantees instant, unconditional cash on demand, no matter whose name is on the account. What the law does require is that the bank acts reasonably, explains itself when asked, and doesn't hold your money indefinitely without a legitimate reason.

What the law says

Ownership of the money isn't in dispute in these cases — the bank isn't claiming it's theirs. What's actually happening is that banks operate under legal duties that sometimes override a customer's instruction to hand over cash immediately, and both the US and UK have built formal channels around exactly this friction.

United States United Kingdom

Reporting trigger Cash transactions over $10,000 require a Currency Transaction Report to the IRS — a disclosure duty, not automatic refusal, but banks often pause the transaction while completing it No fixed statutory threshold — banks set their own internal alert levels based on account history and risk factors
Can they ask why? Yes, particularly for large or unusual withdrawals, as part of fraud and AML due diligence Yes — the Financial Ombudsman has repeatedly upheld banks questioning customers about the purpose of large cash withdrawals as reasonable practice, even when customers found it intrusive
Other valid grounds to refuse Negative balance, legal hold, suspected fraud, documentation issues (e.g. Power of Attorney) Security checks, transaction exceeding a stated daily/branch limit, or a payment that "seems unusual" under the bank's own terms
What they can't do Withhold funds indefinitely with no explanation, or as retaliation unconnected to any legitimate risk basis Refuse unreasonably or fail to explain the basis when the customer asks — FOS decisions require the bank's process to be proportionate, not just present

The Financial Ombudsman Service publishes its case decisions, and a pattern shows up clearly across them: a bank pausing a large or unusual withdrawal to ask questions is treated as legitimate risk management, not a violation of the customer's rights — even when the customer feels it as an intrusion into their own money. What tips a case in the customer's favor is usually delay without explanation, inconsistent information from staff, or the bank failing to follow its own stated process.

Your situation — which one is this?

"My bank won't give me my cash" covers a wide range of very different situations, and what's owed to you changes depending on which one you're actually in.

Covered / expected A large or unusual withdrawal triggered extra questions

If you're withdrawing significantly more than your normal pattern, in an unusual currency, or the transaction otherwise looks out of character for your account, expect the bank to pause and ask about it. This is standard fraud-prevention practice on both sides of the Atlantic, and courts and ombudsmen have consistently upheld it as reasonable, provided the bank moves promptly once you answer.

Covered / expected Documentation is missing (Power of Attorney, business account signatories)

If you're withdrawing on behalf of someone else, or from a business account, the bank is entitled to confirm you have the authority to do so before releasing funds. This isn't optional caution on their part — failing to check can expose the bank to liability if the withdrawal turns out to be unauthorized.

Watch for this Vague refusal with no explanation and no timeline

A bank saying only "we can't process this right now" with no reason, no timeline, and no escalation path is different from a bank running a legitimate check. Ask directly for the specific reason and an expected resolution time — if they can't or won't give you either, that's when to escalate rather than simply wait it out.

Not usually covered A routine, in-pattern withdrawal at your usual branch

A modest, typical withdrawal that matches your normal account activity being refused outright, with no stated reason, would be unusual and is worth pushing back on immediately — this doesn't fit the pattern of the legitimate exceptions above.

Where banks get it wrong (and customers let it slide)

The line between a legitimate security check and an overreach usually comes down to whether the bank is transparent and moves at a reasonable pace, or leaves you in the dark.

Legitimate

"This withdrawal is larger than we'd usually see on this account, so we need to ask a couple of quick questions before releasing it — this is standard fraud protection and shouldn't take long."

Overreach

Refusing to explain why at all, or repeatedly changing the stated reason each time you ask, with no timeline for resolution and no escalation path offered.

Legitimate

Asking for reasonable supporting documentation — a receipt, an explanation of purpose, proof of a related transaction — and releasing the funds promptly once it's provided.

Overreach

Continuing to withhold funds after you've provided everything requested, with no further explanation of what else is needed.

What to say

When a withdrawal is being questioned

You, at the branch or on the phone "I understand you need to run security checks, but can you tell me specifically what you need from me, and give me an expected timeframe for releasing the funds? I'd also like that timeframe confirmed in writing or by email."

If you're not comfortable sharing the purpose

You, setting a boundary "This withdrawal is for a personal matter I'm not comfortable detailing further. Can you confirm what specifically your policy requires before you can release the funds, separate from the purpose itself?"

If the delay drags on with no resolution

You, escalating "It's been [X days] since I requested this withdrawal and I still don't have a resolution or a clear reason. I'd like this escalated to a branch manager today, and I'd like written confirmation of the outcome."

If they still refuse

  1. Ask for the refusal or delay in writing. A specific reason, not a general reference to "security."
  2. Provide reasonable documentation where you can. Receipts, purpose statements, or proof tied to the withdrawal usually resolve most legitimate checks quickly.
  3. Escalate to a branch manager or the bank's internal complaints team if the front-line explanation isn't satisfying or the delay is excessive.
  4. US: file a complaint with the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau at consumerfinance.gov/complaint if the bank's internal process doesn't resolve it.
  5. UK: escalate to the Financial Ombudsman Service if the bank hasn't resolved your complaint within 8 weeks, or has already issued a final response you disagree with.
