Can a free trial charge me if I forgot to cancel it?

Usually yes — the trial was never actually free of terms, just free of an upfront charge.

Kibbo Consumer Desk · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

Yes, this is generally legal — a "free trial" almost always means free of an upfront charge, not free of an agreement to be charged later. When you sign up, you typically agree that the trial converts to a paid subscription automatically unless you cancel, and the company is legally allowed to charge you when that happens, provided they disclosed the terms clearly beforehand: that it would convert, what it would cost, and when. The Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA) is the federal law requiring exactly this disclosure, and it applies nationwide.

What has to be disclosed for the charge to be legitimate

The charge isn't automatically valid just because you clicked "start free trial." Under ROSCA and various state auto-renewal laws, the company generally has to:

  • Clearly state that the trial will convert to a paid subscription if not cancelled
  • Disclose the price you'll be charged and when
  • Get your affirmative consent to these terms — not buried in fine print you'd need to scroll past deliberately
  • Provide a simple way to cancel before the charge happens

If any of these were missing, misleading, or designed to be easy to miss (a classic dark pattern), the charge is more challengeable than a standard, clearly-disclosed one.

California requires an advance reminder — most states don't

Here's a specific, practical right worth knowing: under California's Automatic Renewal Law (as amended, in force since July 1, 2025), if a free trial, gift, or discount period lasts longer than 31 days, the business must send a written or electronic reminder 3 to 21 days before it converts to a paid subscription. If you're a California resident and got charged with no such reminder, that's a specific legal violation to cite.

Most other states don't currently require this specific advance reminder for trials — only for longer-term (one year+) automatic renewals, which is a related but different rule (see our related question on renewal reminders).

When you have a real case to push back

Situation How strong is your case?
Terms were disclosed clearly, price and date included, you just forgot Weak — the charge is likely legitimate. Your best move is requesting a goodwill refund, not disputing it as unauthorized.
The conversion price/date was in fine print, unclear, or contradicted marketing copy Stronger — this may be a disclosure violation, worth disputing directly with the company and, if unresolved, your card issuer.
You're in California, trial was 31+ days, and no advance reminder was sent Strong — a specific, named legal requirement was skipped.

What to do right now

  1. Cancel the subscription immediately, even if you're going to dispute the charge — this stops further charges while you sort out the first one.
  2. Check your original sign-up confirmation email or the trial's terms page for exactly what was disclosed.
  3. Contact the company directly first and ask for a refund, citing the specific disclosure gap if one exists.
  4. If they refuse and you believe the disclosure was inadequate, dispute the charge with your card issuer as a billing error.
  5. Report the pattern to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov if the trial terms seemed deliberately deceptive — this doesn't get your money back directly, but it feeds into enforcement patterns.
