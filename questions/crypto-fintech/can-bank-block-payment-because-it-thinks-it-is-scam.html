Can a Bank Block a Payment Because It Thinks It Is a Scam?

This isn't just allowed — under anti-money-laundering law, it's often required.

Kibbo Editorial Team · Updated October 2026 · 6 min read
Share

Yes — and this isn't just something banks are allowed to do, it's something federal law often expects of them. Under the Bank Secrecy Act, banks are required to monitor for suspicious activity and can face real regulatory consequences if they process transactions that look like fraud or money laundering without taking action. That gives banks both the legal cover and, in many cases, genuine pressure to delay or decline a payment they find suspicious, even a day or two of delay on an otherwise normal-looking transfer.

On top of the general Bank Secrecy Act authority, more than 30 states have passed specific laws letting banks and credit unions delay a disbursement when they reasonably suspect financial exploitation, usually aimed at older or otherwise vulnerable account holders. These delay periods vary by state — commonly somewhere in the range of 15 to 30 business days for an initial hold, sometimes longer with a continued investigation or court order — and the exact rules depend entirely on your state.

What you're entitled to

  • To ask your bank why a payment was delayed or blocked, though the bank isn't always able to tell you everything, especially if a Suspicious Activity Report has been filed.
  • In states with a financial exploitation hold law, to have the delay limited to the period set by that state's law, rather than an indefinite freeze with no process at all.
  • To provide information the bank requests to verify a transaction is legitimate, which is often the fastest way to get a delayed payment released.
  • To escalate through your bank's formal complaint process, and from there to your bank's federal regulator, if you believe a hold was unreasonable or went on too long.

What they can't do

  • Tell you whether it has filed a Suspicious Activity Report about you or the transaction — the Bank Secrecy Act specifically prohibits a bank from disclosing that, even if you ask directly.
  • Hold your funds indefinitely with no legal basis once any investigation is genuinely complete — a hold needs some ongoing basis, whether that's an active SAR-related investigation, a state exploitation-hold statute, or another legal reason.
  • Apply a state's elder or vulnerable-adult financial exploitation hold law to accounts and situations that law doesn't actually cover — those laws are typically scoped to specific age or vulnerability criteria, not every customer.

Exceptions

The general Bank Secrecy Act authority to delay a suspicious transaction applies to any customer, but it isn't usually open-ended — banks typically need to file a Suspicious Activity Report within a set window and resolve the situation rather than sitting on funds forever. The state-specific exploitation-hold laws are different: they usually apply only to older adults or account holders meeting a state's definition of a vulnerable adult, not to the general population, and the permitted delay period, required notifications, and which institutions are covered vary significantly by state. If you don't fall into the category a specific state law protects, your main recourse against a prolonged, unexplained hold is your bank's complaint process and its federal regulator, not a specific statutory time limit.

What to do

  1. If a payment is blocked or delayed, call your bank and ask what information it needs to release it — often a quick verification call resolves it.
  2. If you or the account holder is an older adult and the delay seems to be an exploitation hold, ask whether your state's specific law applies and what the maximum delay period is.
  3. Keep records of the transaction's legitimate purpose (invoices, messages, documentation) to speed up any verification process.
  4. If a hold continues well beyond what seems reasonable with no clear resolution, file a formal complaint with the bank, and if unresolved, with the CFPB or the bank's primary federal regulator.
