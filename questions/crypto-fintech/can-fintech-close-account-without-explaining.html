Can a fintech company close my account without explaining why?

The letter arrives, the reason doesn't — and until recently, that was almost always allowed on both sides of the Atlantic. One side just changed the rules.

Kibbo Consumer Desk·Updated August 2026·8 min read
Short answer

In the US, largely yes — banks and fintechs generally have broad discretion to close an account, and if it's tied to a Suspicious Activity Report, federal law actually forbids them from telling you that's why. In the UK, this changed materially: from 28 April 2026, payment firms (banks, e-money institutions, fintechs) must give 90 days' notice and a specific, detailed written reason, up from the previous standard of 60 days and no explanation at all. The UK change traces directly back to a single high-profile case, which is worth knowing because it explains exactly what the new rule is designed to prevent.

The UK change is genuinely significant and worth understanding in context: it's not a minor tweak. Firms are now expected to write termination notices specific and detailed enough that an ordinary customer can actually understand why they're being closed out — regulators have said they may test this by running notices past customer focus groups. That's a meaningfully higher bar than "we're ending our relationship with you," which was previously considered sufficient.

Your situation — which one is this?

Common and usually not sinister "De-risking" — the account fits a pattern the firm has decided to exit

Banks and fintechs sometimes exit entire categories of customer to reduce compliance workload — freelancers with irregular income, people with frequent international wires, crypto-adjacent businesses, or long-term expats. This isn't about you personally; it's a portfolio-level decision, and it's the single most common reason behind an unexplained closure.

Watch for this A closure that arrives with genuinely no reason and very short notice

If you're in the UK and this happens after 28 April 2026, this itself may not be compliant — ask directly whether the notice meets the new 90-day, written-reason standard. In the US, short or no-notice closures remain broadly permitted, but you're still owed your balance back.

Different territory The closure is tied to a Suspicious Activity Report

If this is the case, in both countries the firm is legally barred from confirming it to you. The first sign is often simply the closure or freeze itself, with support staff repeatedly saying they "can't discuss the specifics" — that phrasing is often the tell.

Worth checking Mixed personal/business use, or activity that doesn't match your stated profile

Running business transactions through a personal account, or a sudden pattern of transfers that doesn't match your account history, are common (and legitimate) triggers for review — not necessarily wrongdoing, but worth understanding since it's fixable going forward with a dedicated business account.

Where firms get it wrong

Legitimate

UK, post-April 2026: "We are closing your account effective [date, 90 days from now] due to [specific, detailed reason]. You can challenge this decision through the Financial Ombudsman Service if you believe it's incorrect."

Overreach

A generic "we are ending our relationship with you" notice with no detail at all, issued with short notice, after the new UK rule takes effect — or a US closure where the firm implies AML suspicion without ever formally filing anything, simply to avoid explaining an ordinary business decision.

What to say

UK, requesting the specific reason

You, in writing to the firm "I've received a closure notice dated [date]. Under the current safeguarding and termination rules, I'm requesting the specific written reason for this closure, and confirmation that the 90-day notice period has been correctly applied."

US, requesting your balance and any available explanation

You, to customer support "I'd like written confirmation of my account closure, the timeline for returning my remaining balance, and any explanation you're able to provide for this decision."

If you believe the closure was wrongful

  1. Get everything in writing. Save the closure notice exactly as received — the wording itself can matter if you escalate.
  2. Diversify before it happens again. Keeping a second account at a different institution means a single closure doesn't cut off your access to money entirely.
  3. UK: escalate through the Financial Ombudsman Service if you believe the closure, notice period, or lack of a specific reason breaches the current rules.
  4. US: if you believe the closure was discriminatory or otherwise improper, complaints can go to the CFPB, the OCC, or your state banking regulator.
