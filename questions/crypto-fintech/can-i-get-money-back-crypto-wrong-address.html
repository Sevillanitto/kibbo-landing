Can I get my money back if I sent crypto to the wrong address?

There's no bank to call and reverse the transfer — but "wrong address" isn't one problem, it's at least three, and only some of them are actually fatal.

Kibbo Consumer Desk·Updated August 2026·7 min read
Short answer

It depends entirely on what "wrong" actually means in your case, and this is the part almost every panicked search misses. If you sent tokens to an address you or an exchange controls, but on the wrong network, there's a genuinely good chance of recovery through the exchange's support process. If you sent it to a valid, unrelated address controlled by a total stranger, the honest answer is that recovery is extremely unlikely — blockchain transactions are irreversible by design once confirmed, and there's no regulator or court that can force a stranger's wallet to release funds simply because you made a mistake. There's no US/UK legal distinction here worth making — the technology itself is the limiting factor in both places, not the law.

What actually determines whether you can get it back

This isn't a legal question so much as a technical one, which is exactly why it surprises people looking for a "rule" that applies. There is no rule — there's only whether the destination address is controlled by someone who can and will help you.

ScenarioRecovery outlook
Sent to the right address, wrong network (e.g. USDT sent as BEP-20 to an ERC-20 deposit address)Often recoverable. If it landed at an exchange's address, their support team can frequently locate and manually credit the deposit, typically within days to a few weeks
Sent to your own wallet, but on a different chain than intendedFrequently recoverable without anyone else's help — if the chain uses a compatible address format (e.g. most EVM chains), the funds may simply be sitting there, accessible by importing the same wallet on that network
Sent to a valid address belonging to an identifiable strangerPossible only through direct contact and voluntary cooperation — there's no technical or legal mechanism to force it, though people occasionally succeed simply by explaining the situation
Sent to a random or non-existent (dead/burn) addressNot recoverable. No one controls the destination, so there's nothing to negotiate and nowhere to send a request

The single biggest mistake people make right after realizing the error is panicking into a worse decision — searching for "crypto recovery services," some of which are outright scams that ask for your seed phrase or an upfront "release fee." No legitimate recovery path ever requires your seed phrase or private keys. If someone asks for either, that's the scam, not the solution.

Your situation — which one is this?

Good odds Wrong network, right exchange

If a block explorer (Etherscan, BscScan, Solscan, Tronscan, depending on the chain) confirms your funds landed at an exchange's known deposit address, contact their support immediately with your transaction hash, the correct memo/tag if applicable, and proof of the sending address. Many exchanges have a defined process for exactly this mistake.

Good odds, no third party needed Wrong network, your own wallet

If the destination address uses a compatible format across chains (common among EVM-based networks like Ethereum, BNB Smart Chain, Polygon, Arbitrum), you may be able to import the same wallet on the correct network yourself and see the funds appear, without needing anyone's permission.

Watch for this Being approached by a "recovery specialist" after posting about your loss publicly

Scammers actively monitor forums and social media for people describing exactly this situation, and reach out offering paid "recovery services." Legitimate recovery never requires upfront payment or your seed phrase — treat any unsolicited offer of help as a scam by default.

Low odds, but not zero A valid address, unknown owner

If you can identify who controls the address (for instance, it belongs to a known service or a person you can trace), a polite, direct request explaining the mistake is the only real path — there's no obligation on their part, but some transfers have been voluntarily returned this way.

Where "recovery services" get it wrong

Legitimate

An exchange's official support team asking for your transaction hash, sending address, and timestamp to manually trace and credit a wrong-network deposit at their own address.

Overreach / scam

Anyone — official-looking or not — asking for your seed phrase, private keys, or an upfront "unlocking fee" before they can "recover" your funds. This is always a scam; no legitimate recovery mechanism works this way.

What to do, step by step

  1. Find the transaction hash and check a block explorer (Etherscan, BscScan, Solscan, Tronscan, or the relevant explorer for your chain) to confirm exactly where the funds landed and whether the address is still active.
  2. If it's an exchange's address, contact their support immediately with the transaction hash, your sending address, the amount, and the correct network/memo details.
  3. If it's your own wallet on the wrong (but compatible) chain, try importing that same wallet using its seed phrase on the correct network — never share that seed phrase with anyone else in the process.
  4. If it's an unrelated, identifiable address, reach out directly and explain the situation, without paying anyone claiming to "facilitate" the return.
  5. If none of the above applies, the funds are very likely permanently lost — treat any paid "recovery" offer from this point forward as a scam.
