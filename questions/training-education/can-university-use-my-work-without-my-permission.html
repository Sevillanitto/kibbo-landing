Can a University Use My Work Without My Permission?

You usually own your coursework the moment you create it — the university doesn't automatically get the rights.

Kibbo Editorial Team · Updated October 2026 · 6 min read
Share

Generally, no — you own the copyright to your own coursework automatically the moment you create it, the same as any other creator would. This surprises a lot of students, who assume that submitting an assignment somehow transfers ownership to the school. It doesn't, under the default rule that applies at most US universities.

University copyright policies across the country are remarkably consistent on this point. The University of California's policy states that registered students generally own the copyrights of their creative works, including theses and dissertations. Rutgers' copyright policy affirms the same. Brown's policy goes further, explicitly stating that student-created works posted in a course online forum are owned by the students and cannot be reproduced or published by instructors without written permission from those students.

What you're entitled to

  • Automatic copyright ownership of your coursework, assignments, papers, and projects the moment you create them — no registration or paperwork is needed for this to apply.
  • To decide whether your work is published, shared externally, used in university promotional materials, or shown to other classes as an example — these all generally require your separate written permission beyond simply submitting the assignment.
  • To be informed if a university wants to use your work in a way that goes beyond internal grading and course administration.
  • A share of any profit if your university commercializes intellectual property you created, under many university IP policies — though this typically applies to significant commercial IP, not ordinary coursework.

What they can't do

  • Publish, reproduce, or publicly share your coursework (online, in promotional material, to other class sections) without obtaining your written permission first, beyond what's needed for grading and internal administration.
  • Treat your enrollment or submission of an assignment as blanket consent to any and all future uses of your work — consent for one use (like grading) doesn't extend to unrelated uses (like marketing).
  • Claim ownership of your coursework simply because you used university facilities like a library, computer lab, or classroom in the ordinary course of your studies — "significant university resources" is a higher bar than normal facility use, and most policies define it that way.

Exceptions

The university does own your work, or has a stronger claim to it, in a few specific situations: if you created it as a paid university employee acting within the scope of that job (a "work made for hire"), if creating it required significant university resources beyond normal coursework, if it was a sponsored, contracted, or commissioned work, or if you signed a separate agreement assigning rights. Financial aid alone doesn't count as "significant university resources" under most policies. Separately, universities almost always retain a limited, non-exclusive license — built into your enrollment, not a separate contract — to use your coursework for grading, accreditation review, and internal academic archiving, which is different from publishing it or using it for promotion.

What to do

  1. Check your specific university's intellectual property or copyright policy — it's usually published by the library or provost's office, and the rules above are the general default, not a universal guarantee.
  2. If an instructor or department wants to use your work beyond grading — as an example for other students, in a publication, or in marketing — ask for this to be documented in writing, including exactly how and where it will be used.
  3. If your work has already been used without your permission, contact your university's copyright office (most larger universities have one) to raise the issue formally.
  4. Keep a personal copy and record of your original work and its submission date — useful evidence if a dispute over authorship or use ever comes up.
