Who is responsible if my parcel says delivered but I never received it?

A tracking status isn't a legal handoff — and the retailer knows that better than most customers do.

Kibbo Consumer Desk · Updated September 2026 · 6 min read

The seller, not the courier. Your contract is with the retailer you bought from — you never agreed to anything with the delivery company they chose to use. In the UK, the Consumer Rights Act 2015 makes this explicit: the retailer remains responsible for the goods until they're physically in your possession, or delivered to a safe place you specifically agreed to — a "delivered" tracking status alone doesn't end that responsibility. In the US, the FTC's Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule places a similar obligation on sellers to actually get the goods to you, with clear rules for what happens if they don't.

What the law says

United States United Kingdom
Legal basis FTC Mail, Internet, or Telephone Order Merchandise Rule (16 CFR Part 435) Consumer Rights Act 2015
Who bears responsibility in transit The seller — required to ship within the stated time, or 30 days if none is stated, and to resolve non-delivery The seller — explicitly responsible for the goods until they're in the consumer's physical possession or an agreed safe place
Default delivery window Within the time stated at purchase, or 30 days if no time was stated Within 30 calendar days unless a different period was agreed
If delivery fails or is delayed Seller must notify you and get consent to the delay, or issue a prompt refund if you don't consent You can request redelivery, or a refund if it wasn't delivered by the agreed date or within a reasonable time
Do you deal with the courier directly? No — the FTC framework places the obligation on the seller, not the delivery company No — Citizens Advice and consumer bodies are explicit that you should never be told to contact the courier yourself

One detail that catches people out: if a courier left the parcel in a "safe place" you specifically authorized at checkout, and it later goes missing from there, your position is weaker — you effectively agreed to that risk. But if it was left somewhere you never specified, responsibility stays squarely with the seller.

Where this actually goes wrong

The seller tells you to "contact the courier yourself"

This is one of the most common ways sellers try to offload a problem that's legally theirs to fix. You have no contract with the courier — the seller chose them, and the seller is the one who has to chase them.

"Delivered" status treated as the end of the conversation

A tracking update isn't proof you received the item — it's proof the courier's system logged something. If you genuinely didn't receive it, that status alone doesn't override your right to a redelivery or refund.

A safe-place instruction you never actually gave

If the courier decided on their own to leave it somewhere — behind a bin, with a neighbor you don't know — without your specific prior instruction, that's different from an authorized safe place, and the seller remains on the hook.

What to say

In the UK:

"Under the Consumer Rights Act 2015, you're responsible for making sure my order is delivered to me at my address or to an agreed alternative safe place. I did not agree to my order being left in an alternative place, and I have not received it. I'd like a redelivery, or a full refund without undue delay."

In the US:

"My order was marked as delivered on [date], but I never received it. As the seller, I'd like you to investigate this with your carrier and arrange either a replacement or a full refund."

If it isn't resolved

  1. Put the request in writing (email creates a clear record), stating whether you want redelivery or a refund.
  2. Check your delivery instructions history — confirm whether you actually authorized a safe place.
  3. If you paid by credit card, a Section 75 claim (UK, for purchases £100-£30,000) or a card dispute/chargeback can recover your money directly from the card issuer.
  4. In the UK, escalate to the Retail Ombudsman if the retailer won't act; in the US, file a complaint with the FTC if a seller isn't complying with shipping and refund obligations.
