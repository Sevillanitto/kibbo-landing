Who pays if a courier damages my package?

The seller sorting things out with their courier is their problem to solve — not yours to referee.

Kibbo Consumer Desk · Updated September 2026 · 5 min read

The seller does — at least as far as you're concerned. Under the UK's Consumer Rights Act 2015, you have a sale of goods contract directly with the retailer, and it's up to them to put things right when a purchase arrives damaged, whether or not it was technically the courier's fault. The retailer can then separately investigate and pursue their own courier over the damage if the courier's contract with them requires proper handling — but that's a business-to-business matter that happens after you've already been made whole, not something you need to negotiate.

What the law says

United States United Kingdom
Legal basis State consumer protection and warranty law — the seller's obligation to deliver goods in the condition sold isn't discharged by carrier damage Consumer Rights Act 2015 — sale of goods contract is with the retailer, who must address the issue regardless of whether it happened in transit
Who you deal with The seller — not the shipping carrier The retailer — not the courier
What you're entitled to A replacement, repair, or refund, depending on the seller's return policy and applicable state law A repair, replacement, price reduction, or refund, following the standard remedy hierarchy under the Act
Seller's recourse against the courier A separate commercial matter based on the seller's own contract with the carrier Same — if the courier breached its handling obligations under its contract with the retailer, that's the retailer's claim to pursue, not yours

The practical upshot: don't let a seller redirect you toward the courier's own damage claims process. Some carriers do have consumer-facing claims systems, but you're not obligated to use them, and doing so can sometimes muddy your stronger, more direct claim against the seller.

Where this actually goes wrong

The seller says "that's a courier issue, file a claim with them"

This shifts a burden that's legally the seller's onto you. You're entitled to push back and insist the seller handle it directly.

Reporting the damage too long after delivery

The longer you wait, the harder it becomes to prove the damage happened in transit rather than afterward — report and photograph as soon as you notice it, ideally the same day.

Accepting a partial resolution because "it's not the seller's fault"

Fault doesn't determine your entitlement here — the seller's obligation to deliver goods in good condition doesn't depend on who's to blame for the damage.

What to say

"My order arrived damaged on [date] — I've attached photos of the item and packaging. As my contract is with you as the seller, I'd like a [replacement / refund] arranged directly, rather than being asked to pursue this with the courier myself."

If it isn't resolved

  1. Take clear photos of both the damaged item and the packaging as soon as you notice it — the packaging condition can help establish where the damage occurred.
  2. Report it to the seller in writing, keeping every message as a record.
  3. If the seller refuses to act, a card chargeback or Section 75 claim (UK) is a direct path to recovering your money.
  4. Escalate to the Retail Ombudsman (UK) or your state consumer protection office (US) if the seller won't resolve it.
