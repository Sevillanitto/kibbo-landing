Which US airline performed worst in 2026? We ranked all 9 on the DOT's own data.

Spirit had the worst on-time rate of any major US carrier in the first half of 2026, and the worst cancellation rate by a wide margin — a decline that ended in the airline's complete shutdown. Allegiant came out on top.

Kibbo Investigations · Published September 2026, updated September 2026 · 11 min read
Update: Spirit Airlines ceased all operations on May 2, 2026, and began liquidation after failing to secure a federal bailout — its second bankruptcy filing since 2024. We've corrected this piece to reflect that and added a full breakdown of Spirit's decline month by month, plus a year-over-year comparison for every carrier.

Every US airline publishes its own version of how good its service is. The Department of Transportation publishes a different version — one built from mandatory monthly filings, not marketing copy. We pulled the DOT's most recent Air Travel Consumer Report, covering January through June 2026, and combined four of its metrics into a single ranking: on-time arrivals, flight cancellations, mishandled baggage, and involuntary bumping.

Spirit Airlines finished last, and by a wide margin. It posted the worst on-time rate of the nine major carriers — 59.84%, nearly 21 points below Alaska, the best performer — and the worst cancellation rate of any carrier, at 7.47% of scheduled flights, more than triple the next-worst airline. Allegiant Air finished first, helped by the lowest cancellation rate and lowest baggage mishandling rate in the group, even though it wasn't the most punctual airline outright.

The combined ranking

We ranked each airline 1st to 9th in each of the four categories, then averaged the four ranks to produce a single composite score. Lower is better.

Who won each individual category

The composite score rewards consistency, which hides how differently airlines get to a good or bad ranking. Some carriers win by dominating one metric; others, like Allegiant, win by never being the worst at anything.

Allegiant's composite #1 finish doesn't come from winning outright anywhere except baggage and bumping — it wasn't the most punctual airline in the group. It won by avoiding bad outcomes everywhere else, which is a different (and arguably more useful for a nervous flyer) kind of reliability than topping any single metric.

Where each airline actually lost or gained ground

Allegiant's top position doesn't come from being the most punctual — it wasn't. Alaska Airlines had the best on-time rate in the group, at 80.51%. Allegiant won on consistency across the other three categories: it cancelled fewer flights than anyone else, mishandled the least baggage, and recorded zero involuntary bumpings for the period.

Spirit's result is driven almost entirely by two categories, and both point to the same underlying story: the airline was in terminal decline throughout H1 2026. On May 2, 2026, Spirit ceased all operations and began an orderly wind-down after failing to secure a $500 million federal bailout — its second bankruptcy filing in under two years. That's why Spirit's on-time data simply stops after May and shows "N/A" for June in the DOT's tables: there were no more Spirit flights to report.

American Airlines and Frontier Airlines round out the bottom three. American's weakest area was its cancellation rate (3.32%, more than double Delta's), while Frontier's outlier is bumping — its involuntary denied-boarding rate of 1.42 per 10,000 passengers was the highest of any carrier in the group, more than double American's (the next-worst) and dramatically higher than Delta and Allegiant, which recorded zero involuntary bumps for the period.

Spirit was already the worst-performing major carrier in January, before its bankruptcy filing became public, and never once matched the industry average in any month it operated. March was its low point — under 50% on-time, nearly 25 points below the industry that month — coinciding with the period the airline was actively negotiating creditor and bailout terms.

How the numbers changed from 2025 to 2026

Year-over-year comparisons separate airlines having a genuinely bad half-year from airlines that are simply, consistently mediocre. We calculated both the raw percentage-point change and the relative percentage change for every carrier's cancellation rate — they tell different stories, and conflating them overstates or understates what actually happened.

Every major carrier's cancellation rate got worse in H1 2026 versus H1 2025 — industry-wide, the cancellation rate rose from 1.57% to 2.23%. But the shape of that decline varies enormously. JetBlue's relative increase (+175%) is larger than Delta's, Alaska's, or United's, even though JetBlue's absolute rate (2.86%) is still lower than Spirit's or American's. Spirit is the outlier by both measures at once — the only carrier where cancellations more than quintupled.

On-time performance moved in both directions: Alaska (+2.79 points), Delta (+0.63), American (+1.03), and Frontier (+2.05) all improved year over year, while Southwest (-4.15), Allegiant (-2.11), JetBlue (-3.47), and Spirit (-18.50) got worse. Spirit's decline dwarfs every other carrier's by an order of magnitude.

How much worse is the worst airline, exactly?

Composite ranks and percentages can undersell how large these gaps actually are in practice. A few direct comparisons:

  • Spirit vs. Alaska (on-time): 59.84% vs. 80.51% — a 20.67 percentage-point gap. Roughly 1 in 5 more Spirit flights arrived late compared to Alaska, relative to total operations.
  • Spirit vs. Allegiant (cancellations): 7.47% vs. 0.90% — Spirit's cancellation rate was 8.3 times Allegiant's.
  • Frontier vs. the next-worst carrier (bumping): 1.42 per 10,000 vs. American's 0.52 per 10,000 — Frontier's involuntary bumping rate was 2.7 times the next-highest carrier, and infinitely higher than Delta's or Allegiant's zero.
  • Industry-wide cancellations, H1 2026: 2.23% (marketing carrier basis), up from 1.57% in H1 2025 — a 42% relative increase across the industry as a whole, not just at the bottom of the ranking.

What this ranking doesn't tell you

A composite score built from four DOT metrics is useful, but it has real limits worth stating plainly:

  • It doesn't tell you whether your specific flight, route, or airport will be on time — these are airline-wide averages, and performance varies enormously by route and time of day (Table 3 in the DOT report shows on-time rates by hour, and evening flights are consistently worse across every airline).
  • It doesn't measure price, seat comfort, customer service quality, or how an airline treats you when something does go wrong.
  • Airline-wide averages can hide large differences between a carrier's own hubs — an airline can be reliable at its main hub and much worse at a smaller focus city.
  • It reflects six months of data, not a full year, and Spirit's mid-period shutdown means its "H1 2026" figure only reflects five months of actual operations, not six.
  • Marketing-carrier figures (used throughout this ranking) include flights operated by branded codeshare partners, which can perform differently than the mainline carrier itself.

How this was calculated

Source: the DOT Office of Aviation Consumer Protection's Air Travel Consumer Report, August 2026 edition, which reports on flight operations and complaints from June 2026 and year-to-date figures for January through June 2026.

We used four tables directly from that report, all covering the nine US carriers required to report data (each holding at least 0.5% of total domestic scheduled-service passenger revenue):

  • On-time arrival rate — Table 1C, year-to-date ranking by reporting marketing carrier.
  • Cancellation rate — Table 6B, year-to-date ranking by reporting marketing carrier.
  • Mishandled baggage rate — year-to-date ranking by reporting marketing carrier, bags mishandled per 100 enplaned.
  • Involuntary denied boardings — year-to-date ranking by reporting marketing carrier, per 10,000 enplaned passengers.

For each metric, airlines were ranked 1st (best) through 9th (worst). The four ranks were averaged to produce each airline's composite score; a lower score means a better overall record across all four categories equally weighted. We didn't weight any single category more heavily than the others — a reader who cares more about punctuality than baggage handling should look at the individual columns in the table above rather than the composite alone.

Monthly on-time figures come from Table 1B of the same report. Year-over-year comparison figures come directly from the same-period columns DOT publishes alongside each YTD table (Tables 1C and 6B), not from a separate calculation against last year's report.

One limitation worth stating plainly: this reflects six months of data, not a full year, and covers domestic operations by marketing carrier (which for some airlines includes regional codeshare partners operating under their brand). We'll revisit this ranking once full-year 2026 data is available — Spirit will no longer appear in that edition, since it ceased operating entirely partway through the year.

What this means if you're booking a flight

None of this tells you whether a specific flight will be on time — DOT data is an airline-wide average, not a prediction for your route. But if punctuality and cancellation risk matter more to you than price on a close call between carriers, this is the most current independent comparison available, and it updates monthly.

If you've already been affected by a cancellation, denied boarding, or long delay, US airlines don't owe you the same fixed cash compensation as EU or UK carriers — but you may be entitled to a refund or specific protections depending on what happened. If your flight was booked on Spirit and never operated because of the shutdown, your recovery options depend on how you paid — check your card issuer's dispute process and any travel insurance separately, since Spirit itself is no longer processing customer service requests.
