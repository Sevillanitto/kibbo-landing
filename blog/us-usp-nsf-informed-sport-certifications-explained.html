USP, NSF, and Informed Sport: What Each Supplement Certification Actually Verifies

A wall of certification logos on the supplement aisle can look interchangeable. They test for genuinely different things, and picking the right one for your situation matters more than just seeing a seal.

The baseline every legitimate supplement should already meet

Before any of these certifications come into play, every legally sold supplement manufacturer is required to follow FDA Good Manufacturing Practices (GMP) — a legal baseline, not a voluntary certification. Third-party certification goes further: it's an independent, outside body verifying claims that go beyond what GMP alone confirms.

USP Verified Mark: does the bottle contain what the label says?

The USP Verified Mark, from the nonprofit US Pharmacopeia, focuses on whether a supplement contains the ingredients listed on the label, in the declared potency and amounts, and that it doesn't contain harmful levels of specified contaminants like heavy metals, pesticides, or microbes. USP tests roughly 40 products a year under this program and conducts ongoing facility audits, not just a single sample test. It's the mark most often recommended by pharmacists and dietitians for general quality assurance.

NSF Certified for Sport: does it contain anything banned in sport?

NSF Certified for Sport builds on a manufacturing-quality baseline (NSF/ANSI 173) and adds screening for roughly 280 substances banned by major athletic organizations, including stimulants, steroids, diuretics, and masking agents. It's recognized by the NFL, MLB, NHL, and other major leagues and is aimed specifically at athletes concerned about failing a drug test due to a contaminated supplement, not just general quality.

Informed Sport: was every single batch actually tested?

Informed Sport takes a stricter approach on one specific dimension: it tests every single manufactured batch of a certified product against the WADA (World Anti-Doping Agency) prohibited list before that batch goes to market, rather than periodic sampling. Certified batches and their lot numbers are published so an athlete can check the exact bottle in their hand against the tested batch record — a level of batch-specific transparency the other programs don't offer in the same way.

Which one matters for your situation

  • General quality and label accuracy for everyday use: USP Verified is the most established, broadly recognized option
  • Playing a drug-tested sport, at any level from school to professional: NSF Certified for Sport or Informed Sport — for many governing bodies this isn't optional
  • Wanting deeper, independent comparative data across a whole product category: a ConsumerLab subscription can add value beyond any single certification mark, though it's a paid service, not a free certification

What none of these certifications mean

No certification proves a supplement's health claims are true, and no supplement can legitimately claim to be "WADA approved" or "endorsed by" any anti-doping body — WADA and national anti-doping organizations do not approve or endorse specific products, and any label claiming otherwise is making a false statement on its face.

What this means practically

  • USP Verified answers "is this what the label says it is," not "does this work as advertised."
  • NSF Certified for Sport and Informed Sport both screen for banned substances, but Informed Sport's every-batch testing model is the more rigorous of the two on that specific point.
  • A label claiming WADA approval or endorsement is a false claim by definition, since no such endorsement exists.
  • None of these certifications substitute for checking a product's actual health claims against real evidence.
