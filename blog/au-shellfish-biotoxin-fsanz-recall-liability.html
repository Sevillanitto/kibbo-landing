Shellfish Biotoxin Outbreaks: FSANZ Recalls and Restaurant Liability

Marine biotoxins that build up in mussels, oysters, and other bivalves survive normal cooking temperatures entirely — meaning the only real defense is auditing your supplier's harvest and cold chain controls before the product reaches your kitchen.

Why Cooking Doesn't Solve This Risk

Marine biotoxins — including those responsible for paralytic shellfish poisoning (PSP), diarrhetic shellfish poisoning (DSP), and amnesic shellfish poisoning (ASP) — accumulate in filter-feeding bivalves like mussels, oysters, clams, and scallops when the animals feed on toxin-producing algae during a harmful algal bloom. Unlike most bacterial food safety risks, these toxins are heat-stable: normal cooking, steaming, or grilling does not destroy them. This means the entire risk-control burden sits upstream, at the harvest and supply stage — a restaurant kitchen cannot cook its way out of a biotoxin problem once contaminated product is on the premises.

How FSANZ Recalls Work in Practice

When biotoxin levels in a harvest area or product batch exceed safe limits under the Food Standards Code, the relevant state or territory food authority — coordinating with Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) — issues a recall or harvest-area closure. You can check the current, live FSANZ Food Recalls list directly before deciding your next step. Recalls are typically communicated through FSANZ's food recall system and state food authority notices, and a restaurant sourcing shellfish should treat any such notice as time-critical: product already in the kitchen from an affected harvest area or supplier batch needs immediate removal from the menu and stock, regardless of whether any customer has yet reported symptoms.

Auditing Your Supply Chain

  • Confirm your shellfish supplier sources from harvest areas under active biotoxin monitoring programs, not just areas with a general good reputation.
  • Request documentation of the specific harvest area and date for each batch — generic "fresh, local" claims aren't sufficient traceability.
  • Verify the cold chain was unbroken from harvest through delivery — biotoxin risk is about harvest-time contamination, but temperature abuse afterward compounds other risks (like bacterial growth) on top of it.
  • Subscribe directly to your state food authority's recall and biotoxin alert notifications rather than relying solely on your supplier to inform you proactively.
  • Build a same-day removal protocol so that when a recall notice arrives, affected stock comes off the menu and out of storage immediately, not at the end of a service period.

Liability Under the Australian Consumer Law

Beyond food safety regulation itself, a restaurant serving contaminated shellfish that causes illness may face liability under the ACL's consumer guarantees — specifically the guarantee that goods (including food) are of acceptable quality, meaning safe, durable, and free from defects a reasonable consumer would expect. A customer harmed by biotoxin poisoning has grounds to pursue compensation for medical costs and related losses under this guarantee, independent of any separate negligence claim. Maintaining thorough supplier documentation and recall-response records is exactly what supports a restaurant's position if such a claim arises — showing the business exercised reasonable diligence in its supply chain, even though the ultimate contamination originated upstream.
