Reporting Mail Theft: Why a USPIS Report Beats a Regular Police Report

Mail theft carried by USPS is a federal felony, investigated by its own dedicated federal law enforcement agency — and the report it produces carries legal weight a merchant's automated "delivered" denial doesn't easily survive.

Federal Jurisdiction — But Only for USPS Mail

Theft or receipt of stolen mail is a federal crime under 18 U.S.C. § 1708, and the US Postal Inspection Service (USPIS) is the federal law enforcement agency responsible for investigating it. This matters specifically for items sent through the US Postal Service — theft of a package shipped by a private carrier like FedEx or UPS generally falls under state or local criminal jurisdiction instead, unless it's part of a broader interstate mail or wire fraud scheme. Know which carrier delivered your item before deciding who to report to — reporting private-carrier theft to USPIS as if it were postal mail theft will send your report to the wrong agency.

Building a Report That's Actually Useful

  1. Secure your carrier tracking logs showing delivery timestamps and any available location data before they become harder to access.
  2. Preserve any video surveillance footage covering the theft window — doorbell or security camera footage is often the single most useful piece of evidence.
  3. Keep your seller invoice showing the item description, value, and any serial numbers.
  4. File a formal mail theft report directly with USPIS through their official reporting channel.
  5. If the theft appears to involve postal personnel specifically (rather than a third party stealing after legitimate delivery), that's a separate report to the USPS Office of Inspector General, not USPIS.

Why This Report Carries Extra Weight

Filing a false statement in a federal report is itself a separate federal crime under 18 U.S.C. § 1001 — which is precisely why merchants, card issuers, and insurers treat a sworn USPIS incident report differently from a simple customer complaint. When a merchant's automated system denies a claim because tracking shows "delivered," presenting an official USPIS report reframes the dispute: it's no longer just your word against a tracking scan, it's a documented federal crime you've sworn to under real legal consequence for dishonesty.

This distinction also matters for insurance: homeowner's and renter's policies covering off-premises property theft frequently require an official police report or a USPIS case number as a prerequisite before they'll even begin adjusting a claim — not simply your description of what happened.
