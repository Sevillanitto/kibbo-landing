The Subscription Price Increase You Didn't Notice: What Happens When Your $9.99 Plan Becomes $19.99

The company didn't cancel your subscription. It quietly changed what you're paying for it. Before calling the new charge unlawful, compare it against the promotional terms, renewal language, notices, and billing record.

A Higher Charge Does Not Automatically Mean an Illegal Charge

Subscription pricing can change for legitimate reasons: a promotional period can expire, a discounted introductory rate can end, or the contract can provide for a later price. The key question is whether the amount charged matches the terms and notices that applied to your subscription.

Do not assume that every price increase requires a particular federal notice period. Notice requirements can depend on the contract, state law, the type of service, and the circumstances of the change.

Start With the Price You Actually Agreed To

Pull together the documents from the moment you subscribed:

  1. The original advertisement or offer.
  2. The checkout page or order confirmation.
  3. The subscription terms in force when you signed up.
  4. Any renewal or price-change communication.
  5. Your billing statements showing the old and new amounts.

Then build a simple timeline: original price → promotional period → renewal date → notice → new charge.

Promotional Pricing Is Where Many Surprises Begin

A $9.99 introductory price may be valid for a limited period even if the regular price is substantially higher. The important distinction is whether the offer clearly communicated the transition and whether the later charge matches the disclosed terms.

Look specifically for language such as "introductory price," "first month," "for the first 12 months," "renews at," or similar renewal language.

Check the Statement Against the Contract

Compare the exact amount billed with the price in the agreement and any later notice. Check the billing frequency too: a $19.99 monthly charge is different from a $19.99 annual charge, even when the number looks familiar.

  • Was the old price promotional?
  • When did the promotional period end?
  • Was the renewal price disclosed?
  • Was a price-change notice actually sent?
  • Does the current charge match the applicable terms?

If the New Price Does Not Match What You Agreed To

Contact the merchant in writing and identify the exact charge, the price you understood you had agreed to, and the document or notice supporting your position. Ask the company to explain the contractual basis for the new amount and request correction or a refund if the charge is inconsistent with the agreement.

If the dispute is not resolved, keep the complete billing record and consider the appropriate complaint or payment-dispute process. Do not characterize a lawful promotional expiration as an unauthorized charge merely because the new price is higher.

What This Means for You

  • Save the original promotional offer.
  • Record the regular renewal price before subscribing.
  • Keep renewal and price-change emails.
  • Compare every new charge with the applicable terms.
  • Dispute only when you have a factual basis for saying the charge is incorrect or unauthorized.
