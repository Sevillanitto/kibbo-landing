Your Used Car Has Problems After You Buy It: What Should You Do First?

A noise, a warning light, a problem that wasn't there during the test drive. What you do in the first few days matters more than how frustrated you feel — here's the order that actually protects your position.

Step 1: Document the problem immediately

Write down exactly what's wrong, when you first noticed it, and the current mileage. Take photos or video of any visible issue or warning light. The specific date matters — the closer a problem surfaces to the purchase date, the stronger an argument that it existed (or was likely to occur) at the time of sale.

Step 2: Check your contract and warranty status before doing anything else

Find your Buyers Guide and sales contract, and confirm whether you bought "As Is" or with a warranty (dealer, manufacturer, or third-party). If a warranty applies, check its specific terms, mileage limit, and whether repairs must be done at an authorized shop to stay valid. This single check determines almost everything about your realistic options going forward.

Step 3: Preserve every communication from here on

Save every email, text, and note from any call with the dealer, including dates and names. If you call rather than email, send a brief follow-up email afterward confirming what was said — this converts a verbal exchange into a written record you can actually point to later.

Step 4: Get an independent diagnosis

Before assuming the dealer's own diagnosis is complete or accurate, get an independent mechanic to assess the problem, especially if you're not confident the dealer will be objective about a fault in a car they just sold you. A written, independent diagnosis is far stronger evidence than a dealer's own assessment if the dispute escalates.

Step 5: Calculate your actual costs

Get a repair estimate, and separately track any other costs the problem has caused — a rental car, towing, lost time. Keep every receipt. This total is what you'll eventually be asking the dealer (or a warranty provider) to address.

Step 6: Raise it with the dealer formally, in writing

Contact the dealer with a clear, factual written complaint: what's wrong, your evidence, and what resolution you want (repair, refund, or warranty coverage). Give a specific, reasonable deadline for a response. If the dealer is a member of The Motor Ombudsman's accredited scheme or a similar body, note that this is available if the direct approach doesn't resolve things.

What this means practically

  • The order matters: document first, then check your warranty/contract status, before contacting the dealer — this avoids giving away information or admissions before you understand your own position.
  • An independent diagnosis carries more weight than the selling dealer's own assessment of a problem with the car they just sold you.
  • Converting verbal conversations into written follow-up emails is a simple habit that meaningfully strengthens your position later.
  • A clear, written complaint with a specific requested resolution and deadline gets better results than a general complaint about being unhappy.
