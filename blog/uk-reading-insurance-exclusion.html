"Not Covered": How to Read an Insurance Exclusion Before Giving Up

"Not covered" can mean three genuinely different things — and only one of them is actually final.

A genuine exclusion

An exclusion removes an entire category of loss from the policy — flood damage on a standard contents policy, or wear and tear on almost any policy, are typical examples. If your situation clearly and specifically falls within a genuine exclusion's wording, there's usually no basis to challenge coverage itself, though the insurer's handling of the claim can still be questioned.

A condition that wasn't met

A condition is different from an exclusion — it's a requirement you had to meet for coverage to apply, such as maintaining a working alarm system or reporting an incident within a specific timeframe. A missed condition can be genuinely fatal to a claim, but it's worth checking whether the condition was actually breached, or only arguably breached.

A debatable interpretation

Sometimes an insurer applies a broad reading of an exclusion or condition to a situation that only loosely fits it. This is the category most worth challenging — get the exact clause wording, compare it word-for-word against your specific facts, and identify precisely where the insurer's interpretation and your situation seem to diverge.

How to tell them apart

Locate the actual clause cited, not just the summary given in the decision letter. Read the full definition section too — many policies define ordinary words narrowly, and a term like "accidental" or "sudden" can carry a specific meaning that changes whether the exclusion genuinely applies.

Don't accept "not covered" as the end of the conversation

If, after checking, your situation looks like a debatable interpretation rather than a clear-cut exclusion, that's exactly the basis for a formal complaint — and if unresolved, ultimately for the Financial Ombudsman Service to review.
