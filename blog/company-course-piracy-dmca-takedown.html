Your Course Got Pirated on Udemy or Skillshare: The DMCA Takedown Process

A properly filed DMCA takedown notice creates a real legal obligation for the platform to act — a vague report through a generic "report content" button often doesn't.

Why a Formal Notice Works Better Than a Generic Report

Under the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), online platforms that host user-uploaded content — including course marketplaces like Udemy and Skillshare — generally qualify for "safe harbor" protection from copyright liability, but only if they act promptly on properly formatted takedown notices. This legal structure creates real leverage for a course creator: a notice that meets the DMCA's specific requirements obligates the platform to remove the infringing content expeditiously to preserve its own safe harbor status, whereas a vague complaint through a generic support form has no equivalent legal weight and often gets deprioritized.

What a Valid DMCA Notice Must Include

  • Your physical or electronic signature (a typed name at the end of an email or form is generally accepted as an electronic signature).
  • Identification of the specific copyrighted work you claim is infringed — the exact course, by title, and where the original legitimately exists (your own course listing).
  • Identification of the specific infringing material and its exact location (the direct URL of the pirated listing, not just "somewhere on your platform").
  • Your contact information — address, phone number, and email.
  • A statement that you have a good faith belief the use is not authorized by the copyright owner, its agent, or the law.
  • A statement, made under penalty of perjury, that the information in the notice is accurate and that you're authorized to act on behalf of the copyright owner.

Most major platforms have a dedicated DMCA/copyright agent contact, separate from general support, published in their terms of service or a dedicated copyright policy page — send your notice there specifically, referencing the DMCA by name.

Locating Pirated Copies

Beyond simply searching your course title, check for pirated content using distinctive phrases from your course description or unique slide text, reverse image search on distinctive thumbnails or course art, and dedicated piracy-monitoring services if you have a substantial catalog worth protecting proactively. Pirated versions are often reuploaded under a different title specifically to evade simple title searches, so distinctive-content searches tend to be more effective than title matching alone.

Beyond the Takedown: Pursuing Damages

A DMCA takedown removes the infringing content but doesn't itself recover money — pursuing damages against the infringing uploader (as opposed to the platform, which is generally protected once it complies with your notice) typically requires identifying the actual infringer, which platforms may only disclose under legal process, and then pursuing a separate copyright infringement claim. For most independent creators, the takedown itself — stopping the ongoing loss — is the practical priority; pursuing damages against an anonymous or judgment-proof individual infringer is often not cost-effective, though it remains a legal option where the infringer can be identified and is worth pursuing.
