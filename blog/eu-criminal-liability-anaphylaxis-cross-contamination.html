Criminal Liability for Anaphylaxis: Why Kitchen Staff Face Real Legal Risk

When a disclosed allergy is mishandled and a customer suffers serious harm or dies, the legal exposure for a restaurant's managers and chefs can go beyond a civil damages claim into criminal negligence territory.

Civil Damages Are Not the Only Exposure

Most restaurant operators think of an allergic reaction incident primarily as a civil liability issue — a compensation claim for medical costs and damages. Across EU jurisdictions, that's only part of the picture. Where a customer's allergy was disclosed and the restaurant's negligence in handling that disclosure caused death or serious bodily harm, criminal law concepts of negligent homicide or negligent bodily injury (the specific terminology and thresholds vary by national criminal code, but the underlying principle — that a duty of care was breached with serious foreseeable consequences — is common across the bloc) can apply to the individuals responsible, not just the business as an entity. This can mean a chef, a manager, or whoever failed to act on the disclosed information facing personal criminal charges, separate from any civil claim against the restaurant itself.

How Cross-Contamination Is Proven

In these cases, the technical question of how an allergen reached a dish it wasn't supposed to be in — cross-contact from a shared cutting board, fryer oil, or utensil — becomes the central evidentiary issue. Investigators and expert witnesses typically reconstruct the kitchen's actual workflow at the time: which equipment was used for which dishes, whether it was cleaned between uses, and whether staff followed (or had access to) any documented allergen-handling procedure. A restaurant with no documented protocol, and no record of what specifically happened in the kitchen that shift, is in a far weaker position to demonstrate reasonable care was taken — even if, in fact, staff tried their best in the moment.

The "Allergic Table" Protocol

A growing best practice across EU kitchens — sometimes referred to as an "allergic table" or dedicated allergen-safe preparation protocol — is treating any order flagged with a disclosed allergy as requiring a physically separate preparation process: dedicated, freshly cleaned equipment, a separate preparation surface, and a named staff member accountable for that specific dish from start to service. This isn't just good practice for preventing an incident — it creates the documented, reconstructable process that becomes critical evidence if something goes wrong despite it.

Reducing Both the Risk and the Exposure

  1. Document every disclosed allergy at the point of order, including who received the information and how it was communicated to the kitchen — our Restaurant Allergen Communication Record is built exactly for this.
  2. Implement a dedicated allergen-safe preparation process for any flagged order, with a named responsible staff member.
  3. Train kitchen staff specifically on cross-contact risks, not just on the theoretical list of allergens — the practical failure point is almost always shared equipment, not lack of allergen knowledge.
  4. Keep records of staff allergen training completion, dated and retrievable, since this becomes relevant evidence of the restaurant's standard of care.
  5. If an incident does occur, preserve everything — the dish, packaging, equipment used, and a written timeline — rather than cleaning up before documenting, since this evidence is exactly what an investigation will need.
