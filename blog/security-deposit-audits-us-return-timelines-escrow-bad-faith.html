Security Deposit Audits in the US: Statutory Return Timelines, Escrow Accounts, and Bad-Faith Penalty Frameworks

Share

Your security deposit is legally your money the whole time — states just disagree sharply on how fast a landlord has to prove that, and what happens if they don't.

Deposits Are Held in Trust, But the Deadline to Prove It Varies by Weeks

Under state residential landlord-tenant statutes, a security deposit remains the tenant's money, held by the landlord in a fiduciary capacity — not the landlord's to spend or commingle freely. What varies enormously is the statutory clock: how long a landlord has, after a tenant moves out, to either return the full deposit or send an itemized statement justifying what was kept.

  • California (Civil Code §1950.5): 21 calendar days to return the deposit or send an itemized statement with supporting documentation. Deductions over $125 generally require copies of invoices or receipts. Move-in photo documentation is required for tenancies beginning on or after July 1, 2025, and move-out photo documentation (taken before and after any repair or cleaning work charged to the deposit) is required for any tenancy where possession is returned on or after April 1, 2025.
  • New York (General Obligations Law §7-108): 14 days after the tenant vacates to provide an itemized statement and return any remaining balance. Miss the deadline, and the landlord forfeits the right to retain any portion of the deposit at all — a genuine "use it or lose it" rule. Deposits are capped at one month's rent statewide, and buildings with six or more units must hold deposits in an interest-bearing New York bank account, with the landlord permitted to keep only a 1% administrative fee from the interest earned.
  • Florida (Fla. Stat. §83.49): 15 days to return the deposit if the landlord makes no claim against it, or 30 days to send written notice (by certified mail or, where authorized, email) of intent to claim part of it. The tenant then has 15 days to object in writing. Miss the 30-day notice deadline entirely, and the landlord forfeits the right to impose any claim on the deposit at all, though a separate damages lawsuit against the tenant may still be possible.

None of these deadlines are aspirational — each is the trigger point for a specific statutory consequence if missed.

Ordinary Wear and Tear Can't Be Deducted — But "Bad Faith" Penalties Vary Sharply by State

Every one of these statutes draws the same basic line: landlords can deduct for unpaid rent and for damage beyond ordinary wear and tear (faded paint, minor carpet matting from normal use), but not for the wear and tear itself. Where states genuinely diverge is in what happens when a landlord crosses that line, or misses a deadline, in bad faith.

In California, a court that finds a landlord acted in bad faith can award the tenant up to twice the amount of the deposit wrongfully withheld, on top of the deposit itself, under Civil Code §1950.5(l). In New York, a willful violation carries punitive damages of up to twice the deposit amount under GOL §7-108(g). Massachusetts goes further: under General Laws Chapter 186, §15B, specific categories of violation — including failing to return the deposit within the statutory window, or failing to hold it in a properly disclosed Massachusetts escrow account — trigger mandatory treble (3x) damages plus attorney's fees, and the tenant does not need to separately prove the landlord acted in bad faith once the underlying violation is established. That distinction matters: a $1,500 Massachusetts deposit mishandled improperly can turn into a judgment well over $4,500 once treble damages, interest, and attorney's fees are added, while the equivalent California or New York violation caps out at double the deposit amount.

What This Means Practically

  • Confirm your specific state's return deadline and escrow requirements before assuming a "standard" 30-day rule applies everywhere — it doesn't.
  • If your landlord misses the notice deadline entirely (not just the return deadline), check whether your state treats that as an automatic forfeiture of their right to deduct anything, as New York and Florida both do.
  • Keep every itemized statement, receipt, and communication — the burden of proving a deduction is reasonable typically falls on the landlord, not the tenant.
  • If you're in a state with mandatory multiplied damages for specific violations (like Massachusetts), a technical statutory violation can be worth pursuing even for a modest deposit.
