Fake Job Placement Rates: How Bootcamps Inflate Outcomes and What the FTC Requires

A "99% placement rate" sounds precise. The precision is often the problem — it usually depends entirely on how "placed" is defined, and who gets left out of the count.

Why the Denominator Matters More Than the Number

The FTC prohibits deceptive representations about outcomes in advertising generally, including in education marketing — a claim doesn't need to be literally false to be deceptive; it's enough that it creates a misleading overall impression a reasonable consumer would rely on. Job placement statistics are one of the most commonly manipulated figures in bootcamp and vocational school marketing because the calculation offers several places to quietly shift the result: which graduates count in the total, what counts as a qualifying "placement," and how the timeframe is measured.

Common ways the number gets inflated: excluding students who didn't complete the program from the denominator (so a school with a 40% completion rate can still claim a high placement rate among the minority who finished); counting unpaid internships, unrelated part-time jobs, or self-employment as "placement"; and measuring placement at any point after graduation rather than within a clearly stated window.

Independent Auditing Exists — Ask Whether It Was Used

Some bootcamps voluntarily submit their outcome data for independent audit by a body like the Council on Integrity in Results Reporting (CIRR), which applies a standardized methodology and definitions specifically to prevent the manipulation described above. A bootcamp that cites a placement statistic without disclosing its methodology, denominator, and timeframe — or that hasn't submitted to any independent audit — is giving you a number you can't actually verify.

What to Do If You Were Misled

  1. Save the specific marketing materials that stated the outcome claim — screenshots of the website, saved emails, or a recording of a sales call if one exists.
  2. Request the school's actual methodology for the stated placement rate in writing — a legitimate school should be able to provide this.
  3. Compare the actual outcome you (or people you know) experienced against the specific claim made — a documented, specific gap is stronger evidence than general disappointment.
  4. File a complaint with the FTC directly if you believe the claim was materially misleading — this contributes to the regulatory record even if it doesn't resolve your individual situation immediately.
  5. Pursue a refund request or formal complaint against the school directly, referencing the specific misrepresented claim.
