Is Your Supplement Actually an Unlicensed Medicine? The MHRA's Borderline Products Rule

A sleep aid sold over the counter in every US pharmacy requires a doctor's prescription in the UK. The reason isn't availability — it's how the MHRA classifies "borderline" substances between food and medicine.

What a "borderline product" actually means

The Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA) uses the term "borderline substance" when it's initially unclear whether a product should be classified as a medicine, a food, a device, or something else. The MHRA's Borderline Section makes this determination, and if a product is found to have a medicinal function — meaning it's used to treat, prevent, or diagnose a condition through a pharmacological, immunological, or metabolic effect — it needs a marketing authorisation (a product licence) before it can be legally sold, regardless of how it's marketed or what it's called.

Melatonin: the clearest example

Melatonin is a hormone sold freely as a supplement in the US, Canada, and Australia. In the UK, it's been classified as a Prescription-Only Medicine (POM) since 1995, when the MHRA (then under different legislation) reclassified it under the Medicines Act 1968. The reasoning: melatonin has a direct pharmacological effect on the body's sleep-wake cycle and circadian rhythm — exactly the kind of effect the MHRA associates with a medicine, not a food supplement. Under the Human Medicines Regulations 2012, selling melatonin as a food supplement or over-the-counter product in the UK without a marketing authorisation is a criminal offense.

Why the same ingredient gets treated so differently by country

Classification isn't about the ingredient's inherent safety — it's a regulatory judgment call made by each country's own medicines authority, based on that authority's own criteria for what constitutes a "medicinal function." The US treats melatonin as a dietary ingredient under DSHEA; the UK's MHRA treats it as a hormone with direct pharmacological effects requiring medical oversight. Both frameworks are legitimate within their own systems — but only one applies to a product sold in the UK.

What this means if you're buying from abroad

UK residents can legally bring in a personal supply of melatonin from abroad — up to a three-month supply, in original packaging, genuinely for personal use — but ordering it for delivery to a UK address from an overseas supplement retailer is a different matter, and packages have been seized by Border Force under these rules (see our related article on UK customs seizures for details).

How to check if something you're considering is a borderline product

  1. Check whether the substance has a direct hormonal, pharmacological, or metabolic effect described in its marketing, rather than a general "supports" wellness framing.
  2. Search whether the MHRA has published guidance or a public assessment report classifying that specific substance.
  3. Be cautious with any product marketed in the US as a "natural sleep aid," "hormone support," or similarly strong physiological claim — these categories are more likely to hit UK borderline classification.
  4. If in doubt, a UK pharmacist can usually tell you quickly whether an ingredient is available over the counter, prescription-only, or unlicensed in the UK.

What this means practically

  • A supplement being legal and freely available in the US, Canada, or Australia says nothing about its legal status in the UK.
  • Melatonin specifically requires a UK prescription — it cannot be legally sold as a supplement here, however it's classified elsewhere.
  • Personal import allowances exist for genuine personal use in limited quantities, but ordering commercially from an overseas retailer for UK delivery carries real seizure risk.
  • When a US product's marketing leans heavily on a specific physiological or hormonal effect, that's worth checking against UK borderline rules before assuming it's simply a supplement here too.
