Deposit Disputes in Small Claims Court: Building Your Evidence File, the Formal Demand Letter, and the Burden of Proof

Small claims courts are built to be navigable without a lawyer — but the case still lives or dies on how well you organize your evidence before you file, not on legal argument.

Start With the Formal Demand Letter — Courts Expect to See It

Before filing any small claims case over a withheld deposit, send a formal written demand to the landlord first. This isn't just good practice — many small claims courts either require proof you attempted to resolve the matter directly before filing, or a judge will simply expect to see it as part of a reasonable pre-litigation process. Your demand letter should state clearly: the amount owed, the specific reason it's owed (full non-return, or a dispute over specific deductions), a reasonable deadline to respond, and a note that you intend to pursue small claims court if the matter isn't resolved.

Structuring the Evidence File

Organize your case file in the order a judge will actually want to review it, not the order events happened to you:

  1. The tenancy agreement — establishes the deposit amount and the terms both parties agreed to.
  2. Move-in condition documentation — your signed condition report and dated photos, establishing the baseline.
  3. Move-out condition documentation — your dated photos taken the same standard way, for direct comparison.
  4. The landlord's itemized deduction statement, if one was provided — this is what you're directly rebutting.
  5. Your counter-evidence for each disputed item — professional cleaning receipts if you had the unit cleaned, independent repair quotes if you're disputing an inflated cost, or your move-in photos showing pre-existing damage.
  6. The full written communication timeline — every email, text, or letter, in chronological order, including your formal demand letter and any response.

How Courts Generally Treat Photo Evidence

Courts generally give more weight to photo evidence that's clearly dated and consistently sourced — for example, photos taken with a phone's built-in camera that embeds a timestamp and, where available, location metadata, rather than photos with no verifiable date attached. This doesn't mean informal phone photos are inadmissible — small claims courts are typically far more relaxed about evidentiary formality than higher courts — but a judge deciding between two competing accounts of a property's condition will weigh a consistently dated photo record more heavily than an undated one. If your phone doesn't embed metadata automatically, note the date and what the photo shows directly in your written submission, and be prepared to testify to when and how each photo was taken.

What This Means Practically

  • Always send a formal written demand before filing — it's often expected, and it starts your written evidence trail.
  • Organize your evidence file in the logical order above before your hearing date, not the night before.
  • Prefer photos with embedded timestamp/location data where your device supports it — it's simple, free evidentiary weight.
  • Bring physical or digital copies of everything — don't assume the court will accept a live phone scroll-through as evidence.
