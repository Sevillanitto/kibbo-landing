The Subscription You Thought You Cancelled: What to Do When the Charges Keep Coming

Share

You cancelled. You received confirmation. Three weeks later, the company charged your card again. The strongest response is not another phone call — it is a clean record showing exactly when and how you cancelled.

You Cancelled. So Why Are They Still Charging You?

Recurring subscriptions are designed to keep billing automatically until the customer cancels. The problem starts when the cancellation has already happened — but the charges continue.

The Federal Trade Commission advises consumers to keep a copy of the cancellation request and notes from conversations about when and how the cancellation was made. If a company continues charging after you told it to cancel, the FTC says you can dispute the charge with your credit or debit card company. FTC Consumer Advice.

Your Cancellation Proof Is the Most Important Evidence

Before arguing about the next charge, establish a simple timeline. Your goal is to be able to show:

  1. What you cancelled — the subscription, membership, account, or recurring service.
  2. When you cancelled it — include the exact date and, if available, the time.
  3. How you cancelled — online form, account settings, email, phone, chat, or another method.
  4. What confirmation you received — save the confirmation email, cancellation page, reference number, or screenshot.
  5. What happened afterwards — record every recurring charge that appeared after cancellation.

Do not rely on memory alone. The FTC specifically recommends keeping the cancellation request and notes about conversations, including how and when you cancelled. FTC Consumer Alert.

Build the Evidence Before Contacting the Company

A useful cancellation record is chronological. Put the original subscription confirmation, cancellation request, cancellation confirmation, later charges, and subsequent communications in one place.

  • Original subscription or renewal terms.
  • Cancellation request and confirmation.
  • Screenshots showing the cancelled status, if available.
  • Emails, chat transcripts, or notes from phone calls.
  • Bank or card statements showing charges after cancellation.
  • Refund promises or case/reference numbers.

This matters because a later dispute is much easier to explain when the dates line up clearly: cancelled → confirmed → charged again.

Contact the Company — But Keep the Record Clean

Start with the company that operates the subscription and use its stated cancellation or support process. Explain that the subscription was cancelled on a specific date and identify the charge that occurred afterwards.

Ask for two things in writing: confirmation that the recurring billing has stopped and a refund for any charge that should not have occurred after cancellation.

If you speak to someone by phone, write down the date, time, name or representative identifier if available, what was promised, and any case number. If the company resolves the issue, keep the written confirmation.

If the Charges Continue, Dispute the Charge With Your Card Company

The FTC says that if a company continues charging your credit or debit card after you tried to cancel, you can file a dispute — also called a chargeback — with your card company. You can generally start the process online or by calling the number on the back of your card. FTC Consumer Advice.

For credit-card billing errors, the FTC advises sending a written dispute to the issuer's billing-disputes address and says the notice generally needs to reach the issuer within 60 days after the first statement containing the error was sent. Keep copies of the dispute and supporting evidence. FTC Sample Dispute Letter.

Credit and debit cards do not have identical legal protections, so do not assume that the same deadline or process applies to both. Check your issuer's dispute instructions and act promptly.

What to Include in the Dispute

Make the dispute factual and chronological rather than emotional. Include:

  1. The name of the merchant.
  2. The date and amount of the disputed charge.
  3. The date you cancelled the subscription.
  4. The method you used to cancel.
  5. The cancellation confirmation or other proof you received.
  6. A statement explaining that the charge occurred after cancellation.
  7. Copies of supporting documents, keeping the originals for your records.

The FTC's sample dispute guidance recommends identifying the account, amount, date, and reason the charge is incorrect, and keeping documentation supporting the dispute. FTC Consumer Advice.

Don't Assume Replacing Your Card Solves the Problem

If a subscription keeps charging after you cancelled, changing a card number is not necessarily the complete solution. The FTC specifically warns consumers with recurring subscription problems to dispute unauthorized charges with their card company; simply changing the card number may not be enough in some situations. FTC Consumer Alert.

The safer approach is to create a documented cancellation trail, notify the merchant, monitor the account, and use the card issuer's dispute process when appropriate.

When to Report the Company to the FTC

If you have problems cancelling a subscription or were charged for a subscription you did not agree to, the FTC says you can report the problem at ReportFraud.ftc.gov. You can also report the issue to your state attorney general. FTC Consumer Advice.

The FTC finalized a "Click-to-Cancel" rule in October 2024 that would have required clear disclosures, informed consent, and a simple cancellation mechanism for many recurring subscriptions. The Eighth Circuit vacated those 2024 amendments in 2025, and the FTC's February 2026 final action recodified the Negative Option Rule as it existed before them — so the 2024 Click-to-Cancel requirements are not currently enforceable federal law. The surviving Negative Option Rule, the FTC Act, and state consumer-protection laws may still apply depending on the transaction, so consumers should not assume every subscription dispute is automatically covered in the same way. FTC: Click-to-Cancel Rule announcement.

What This Means for You

  • Save the cancellation request and confirmation immediately.
  • Record exactly when and how you cancelled.
  • Monitor your statements for recurring charges after cancellation.
  • Contact the merchant and request a written resolution.
  • Dispute qualifying charges with your card company promptly.
  • For credit-card billing errors, pay attention to the applicable written-dispute deadlines.
  • Keep every email, screenshot, statement, letter, and case number together.
  • Report persistent cancellation problems or unauthorized subscription charges to the FTC when appropriate.
