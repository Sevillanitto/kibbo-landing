The Used Car Has a Problem After Purchase: What Are Your Rights in the UK?

The exact same fault gives you very different rights depending on one detail: who you bought the car from.

If you bought from a dealer: the Consumer Rights Act 2015 applies

The car must be of satisfactory quality (accounting for its age, price and mileage), fit for purpose, and as described. Three tiers of protection follow a fault:

  • Within 30 days: the short-term right to reject — return the car for a full refund, no need to give the dealer a chance to repair first.
  • Day 31 to 6 months: the dealer gets one attempt to repair or replace (their choice). Faults in this window are presumed to have existed at purchase — the burden is on the dealer to prove otherwise.
  • After 6 months: you can still request a repair or refund, but the burden shifts to you to show the fault was present at the time of sale.

If you bought privately: a much narrower set of rights

Private sales fall largely outside the Consumer Rights Act's "satisfactory quality" and "fitness for purpose" protections — the "buyer beware" principle applies. What still applies: the car must match its description, and the seller must not misrepresent it (for example, lying about accident history, previous owners, or service records). If a private seller knowingly misdescribed the car, that's a misrepresentation claim, not a Consumer Rights Act claim — a different legal route with a different evidence burden.

Building your case either way

Regardless of seller type, the evidence you gather is what determines the outcome: the original advert and any claims made in it, dated messages, an independent diagnostic report describing the fault and when it likely arose, and every receipt or communication from the point you raised the issue. Escalate in writing first — many disputes never need to go further than a well-documented letter.
