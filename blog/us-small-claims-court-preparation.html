Small Claims Court in the US: What You Actually Need Before You File

Share

Having a legitimate complaint isn't enough. Small claims judges hear dozens of cases a day, and the ones that win are almost always the ones that show up organized, not the ones that show up angry.

Confirm the basics first — they vary a lot by state

Small claims monetary limits vary significantly across US states, and there's no single national figure — some states cap claims in the low thousands, others allow considerably more. Jurisdiction is also state- and county-specific, generally tied to where the defendant resides or where the dispute occurred. Check your specific state and county court's rules before assuming your claim qualifies or filing in the wrong location.

The pre-filing step people skip

Many small claims courts expect, and some formally require, that you've made a genuine attempt to resolve the dispute directly before filing — typically evidenced by a demand letter. This isn't just a formality: judges frequently ask directly whether the defendant was given a chance to resolve things before being brought to court, and a documented demand letter answers that question cleanly.

What actually needs to be in your evidence file

  • The contract or agreement itself — the written terms at the center of the dispute, if one exists
  • A clear, dated timeline — what happened and when, in chronological order, not a narrative written from memory the night before your hearing
  • Proof of payment — bank statements, receipts, or transaction confirmations showing exactly what was paid and when
  • Communications — emails, texts, and notes from phone calls showing your attempts to resolve the issue
  • Proof of damages — receipts, repair estimates, or invoices supporting your specific claimed amount, not just a round number

Why organization matters more than eloquence

Small claims court is designed for people without lawyers, and judges are used to hearing both sides present their case directly. What consistently separates a strong presentation from a weak one isn't legal sophistication — it's whether the evidence is organized in a way the judge can follow quickly: chronological, labeled, and directly tied to the specific amount being claimed.

How to prepare before your court date

  1. Confirm your claim amount is within your specific court's small claims limit.
  2. Confirm you're filing in the correct court based on the defendant's location or where the dispute occurred.
  3. Send a demand letter if you haven't already, and keep proof it was sent.
  4. Assemble your evidence in chronological order, with copies for both the court and the other party.
  5. Calculate your total claimed amount with a clear, itemized breakdown you can explain in one or two sentences.
  6. Confirm how the defendant will be formally served with your claim, since improper service can delay or derail your case entirely.

What this means practically

  • Small claims monetary limits and procedures vary by state and county — verify your specific court's rules rather than assuming a nationwide standard.
  • A documented demand letter sent before filing strengthens your case and satisfies a step some courts formally require.
  • Your evidence should be organized chronologically and tied directly to your claimed amount — this matters more than how persuasively you can speak.
  • Proper service of your claim on the defendant is a procedural requirement that can derail an otherwise strong case if it's done incorrectly.
