Your EU Prescription Abroad: Can You Get Your Medicine in Another Country?

You're travelling through Europe and run out of a prescription. Can a pharmacy in another EU country dispense it? The honest answer in 2026 is: it depends heavily on where you are.

The Legal Principle: EU Prescriptions Are Meant to Be Recognized

In principle, EU rules require that a prescription issued in one member state be recognized in another, so you shouldn't need to start from zero with a new local doctor's visit just because you're travelling. In practice, how smoothly this works depends heavily on whether the countries involved are digitally connected for this purpose, or whether the pharmacist has to work from a paper prescription and manually verify it.

ePrescription and eDispensation: Real, But Not Universal Yet

The EU has been building a digital system — ePrescription and eDispensation services under the MyHealth@EU infrastructure — that lets a pharmacist in one member state look up and dispense against a prescription issued electronically in another, without you needing to carry paperwork or have it manually verified. This is a genuinely useful service where it's live. The important caveat: this is being rolled out country by country, not switched on everywhere simultaneously. Under the European Health Data Space Regulation, full cross-border exchange of ePrescriptions across all 27 member states isn't legally required until March 2029 — as of 2026, several countries are still actively connecting their national systems, and coverage between any two specific countries varies.

What a Pharmacy May Need If the Digital System Isn't Available

Where the electronic system isn't yet connected between your home country and the one you're in, you'll typically need: the physical prescription itself (in a legible format, ideally with the medication's international non-proprietary name rather than only a brand name that may not be sold locally), and potentially a translation or the pharmacist's own verification process. Brand names for the same medication frequently differ between EU countries, which is why the generic/scientific name matters more when travelling than the brand name you're used to at home.

Verifying the Pharmacy Itself

Recognition rules aside, it's also worth confirming the pharmacy you're dealing with is legitimate — particularly if you're sourcing medicine online rather than walking into a physical premises. Since 2015, every online pharmacy or retailer legally selling medicines in the EU (plus Norway, Iceland, and Liechtenstein) must display a common EU logo; clicking it verifies the seller against the official national register, rather than just taking the site's own claims at face value.

What This Means Practically

  • Before travelling, check whether ePrescription exchange is active between your home country and your destination — don't assume it is.
  • Carry your prescription with the medication's international non-proprietary (generic) name clearly visible, not just the brand name you know it by.
  • If the digital system isn't connected for your route, expect to need the physical prescription and possibly additional verification — build in extra time rather than assuming an instant dispensation.
  • If you're refused a medication a local pharmacist is uncertain about, ask specifically what documentation would resolve the uncertainty rather than assuming the medication simply isn't available there.
  • If buying from an online pharmacy in the EU, look for and click the EU Common Logo before you order — it's a genuine, independently confirmed verification, not a general information page.
