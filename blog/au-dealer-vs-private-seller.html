Dealer vs Private Seller in Australia: Why the Difference Matters

Two cars, the same price, the same fault six months later — and completely different odds of getting your money back, depending on who sold it to you.

Buying from a licensed dealer

Consumer guarantees under the Australian Consumer Law apply automatically — the car must be of acceptable quality and fit for purpose. Many states and territories add a statutory warranty on top for dealer-sold vehicles under a certain age and mileage threshold (commonly around 10 years / 160,000 km, though exact thresholds vary by state). Dealers must also generally ensure the vehicle is free of undisclosed finance.

Buying from a private seller

Consumer guarantees and statutory warranty schemes don't apply to private sales — the "buyer beware" principle governs. Your main protection is that the seller must not misrepresent the vehicle (false statements about condition, history, or title are a different legal issue, potentially involving misleading conduct rules, separate from the consumer guarantee framework that only covers trader sales).

Auctions sit in between

Buying at auction typically means no cooling-off rights and, if the vehicle is sold on behalf of someone who isn't a licensed dealer, no statutory warranty either — even though an auction can feel like a more "formal" purchase than a private sale.

What to document differently

Dealer purchase: keep the contract and any statutory warranty paperwork — the legal framework does much of the work if something goes wrong. Private purchase: the advert, all seller communications, and a written record of what was said at handover carry far more weight, since there's no statutory guarantee to fall back on.
