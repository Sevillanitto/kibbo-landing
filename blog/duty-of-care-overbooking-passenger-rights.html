Passenger Rights in the U.S.: What the Airline Owes You During Overbooking

Cash compensation is only half the story. While you wait for a replacement flight, the airline has separate, legally distinct obligations to feed you, house you, and keep you connected — and most bumped passengers never claim any of it.

The Myth

"If overbooking forces me to wait overnight for a replacement flight, I just have to pay out of pocket for my own hotel and meals while I wait."

Most passengers focus entirely on the cash compensation question — the 200%/400% payout — and assume anything beyond that is a courtesy the airline may or may not choose to offer. It isn't a courtesy. It's a separate, mandatory obligation.

The Reality

Beyond the statutory cash payout, federal consumer protection standards require airlines to rebook bumped passengers on the next available flight at no extra cost, and to provide a defined set of care services for as long as you're waiting. This is generally referred to as the airline's duty of care, and it is legally distinct from — and payable in addition to — any cash compensation you're owed for the denied boarding itself.

Crucially, duty of care isn't conditional on why you were bumped. Whether the delay was the airline's operational failure or something closer to bad luck, the obligation to keep you fed, housed, and connected while you wait doesn't disappear.

The Hard Data: What Duty of Care Actually Includes

Under U.S. Department of Transportation standards, passengers who are involuntarily bumped from an overbooked flight must be provided with:

  • Free alternate transportation — rerouting on the next available flight, whether with the same airline or a partner carrier under an interline agreement, at no additional charge to you.
  • Meal vouchers — food and beverage vouchers proportional to how long you're waiting at the terminal, not a token gesture regardless of wait length.
  • Overnight hotel accommodation — free lodging plus round-trip ground transportation to and from the hotel, whenever your replacement flight doesn't depart until the following day.
  • Communication access — phone or internet access so you can notify family, work, or onward connections about the delay.

Real Cases: How Duty of Care Plays Out at the Gate

Case 1: Overnight Rebooking

The scenario: You're bumped from an 8:00 PM flight, and the airline's earliest available seat is on an 8:00 AM departure the next morning.

Your rights: The airline must provide a hotel voucher for the night, round-trip shuttle transportation between the airport and the hotel, and meal vouchers covering both dinner that evening and breakfast the next morning — not just one or the other.

Case 2: Gate Staff Claiming They Can't Issue Vouchers

The scenario: During a five-hour daytime wait, gate staff tell you they're unable to print meal vouchers — perhaps a system issue, perhaps simple unwillingness to deal with it.

Your rights: You're entitled to purchase reasonable meals yourself inside the terminal, keep the itemized receipts, and submit them directly to the airline for full reimbursement afterward. "We can't print a voucher right now" is not the same as "you're not entitled to this" — it just shifts who pays upfront.

The Protocol: Asserting Your Duty-of-Care Rights at the Gate

  1. Demand immediate rerouting first. Ask to be placed on the earliest available flight, even one operated by a different airline, if an interline agreement exists between the carriers.
  2. Collect care vouchers on-site, before you leave the gate area. Meal, hotel, and transit vouchers are far easier to obtain in person at the counter than to chase down later by phone.
  3. If vouchers aren't offered, pay and keep every receipt. Reasonable food and lodging costs are reimbursable even when the airline fails to provide vouchers directly — but only if you have itemized proof.
  4. Collect your cash compensation separately. Your mandatory 200% or 400% payout under 14 CFR Part 250 is calculated and owed independently of the accommodation and meal assistance described here — accepting one doesn't substitute for or reduce the other.
