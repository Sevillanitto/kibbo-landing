Administrative Silence: When No Response Actually Means Yes

A building permit application goes three months without a reply. In many EU countries, that silence isn't a dead end — it can legally mean your request was granted. The rules for exactly when that happens are more specific than most applicants realize.

This is a national concept, not an EU-wide rule

Administrative silence — what happens legally when a public authority fails to respond to a request within the required time — is regulated separately by each EU member state's own administrative procedure law, not harmonised at EU level. Spain, France, and Italy are among the countries with well-developed rules on this; the specific deadlines, defaults, and exceptions differ from one country to the next, so always check your own country's specific framework rather than assuming Spain's rules (used here as a detailed example) apply elsewhere.

Spain's default rule: silence usually means yes

Under Article 24.1 of Spain's Ley 39/2015, the general rule for procedures initiated by an applicant is that silence is positive — if the maximum response deadline passes without an explicit decision, the request is deemed granted. The maximum general deadline is three months, extending to six months for procedures involving a hearing or public information stage. This surprises many people, since the common assumption is that no response means rejection — in Spain, that's actually the exception, not the rule.

When silence is negative instead

Spanish law carves out specific exceptions where silence is negative — meaning the request is deemed refused. These generally include procedures where an automatic approval could affect third-party rights, harm the environment, or improperly transfer public assets or public-service functions, along with appeals against a prior administrative act. A useful way to remember the pattern: think about whether an automatic "yes" could affect general interests, third-party rights, or public property — those are the categories most likely to default to negative silence instead.

Why positive silence is a real, usable administrative act

Positive administrative silence isn't just a legal technicality — Spain's Real Academia de Jurisprudencia y Legislación treats it as a genuine, presumed administrative act that concludes the procedure, effective against anyone (not just the specific office that failed to respond). This means it can be relied upon and proven through any legally admissible evidence, including a formal certificate confirming the silence, which the applicant can request from the competent body even though obtaining it isn't mandatory to benefit from the silence itself.

What negative silence actually does — and doesn't do

Negative silence isn't itself a real administrative decision — it's a procedural fiction that exists specifically to let the applicant move forward and file an appeal, rather than being stuck waiting indefinitely for a response that never comes. Because it's only a fiction for this purpose, the administration can still issue an actual explicit decision later, even after the negative-silence deadline has passed, and that late decision can go either way.

How to act on administrative silence in practice

  1. Confirm the specific deadline that applied to your request, and calculate the exact date it expired.
  2. Check whether your specific type of request falls into a negative-silence exception category under your country's law — don't assume the general default applies without checking.
  3. If silence was positive, consider requesting a formal certificate confirming it, especially if you'll need to prove your right to third parties (for example, to a bank or contractor relying on a permit).
  4. If silence was negative, use it to file your appeal or judicial challenge — treat it as the trigger for your next step, not as a final dead end.
  5. Keep your original submission's proof of receipt with its exact filing date — this is what your entire silence calculation depends on.

What this means practically

  • Administrative silence rules vary significantly by EU country — check your own country's specific administrative procedure law rather than assuming a single EU-wide rule.
  • In Spain, positive silence is the general default; negative silence is the carved-out exception for specific categories affecting third parties, the environment, or public assets.
  • Positive silence functions as a genuine administrative act you can rely on and prove, including through an optional formal certificate.
  • Negative silence is a procedural fiction that unlocks your right to appeal — it doesn't prevent the administration from issuing a real decision later.
