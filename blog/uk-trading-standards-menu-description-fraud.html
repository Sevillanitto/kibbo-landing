Trading Standards and Menu Descriptions: Avoiding False Origin Claims

A recent regional sweep found only 2 of 15 shops fully compliant on meat origin claims. Calling something "local" or "free-range" when it isn't is a criminal offense, not a marketing gray area.

The Law Behind Menu Descriptions

Menu and food description accuracy in the UK is enforced primarily under the Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations 2008 (CPUTR), alongside the Food Safety Act 1990 and, for fresh/chilled/frozen meat specifically, mandatory country-of-origin labeling rules requiring the name of the food, the type of meat, and the country of rearing and slaughter. Misleading or unclear labeling under these rules is a criminal offense, not a civil dispute — enforcement sits with local authority Trading Standards Officers, who can issue improvement notices, take formal samples for independent testing by a Public Analyst, refer cases to the National Food Crime Unit, or pursue prosecution in the most serious cases.

What "Local" Actually Has to Mean

A recent regional Trading Standards investigation into meat provenance claims — checking 15 butchers and farm shops across a mix of local authority areas — found only 2 fully compliant. Officers uncovered imported meat marketed as "local" or "free-range" on websites, social media, and in-store signage. Provenance claims like a regional name or "outdoor-reared" must be backed by written confirmation from the supplier — a business can't simply assert a claim it can't document if challenged. Trading Standards guidance is explicit: "locally sourced" can be misleading even when technically true in a narrow sense — meat bought from a local wholesaler that itself imported the product from abroad does not make the meat "local" in the sense a customer would reasonably understand it.

Common Menu Claims That Draw Scrutiny

  • "Fresh" applied to anything previously frozen — a specific and commonly checked claim.
  • "Local," "regional," or a named farm/region when the ingredient's actual supply chain doesn't support that specific claim.
  • "Free-range," "organic," or other production-method claims without supplier documentation to back them up.
  • "Homemade" or "made in-house" when an item is actually bought in pre-prepared.
  • Species substitution — describing one type of fish or meat while serving a cheaper substitute, a category Trading Standards treats especially seriously as straightforward economic fraud on the customer.

Protecting Your Menu

  1. Before printing any origin, freshness, or production-method claim on your menu, get written confirmation from the specific supplier backing that exact claim.
  2. Review menu claims whenever you change suppliers — a claim that was accurate with your old supplier may not transfer automatically to a new one.
  3. Keep supplier invoices and country-of-origin paperwork on file and readily retrievable, since this is exactly what an inspecting officer will ask to see.
  4. Avoid vague claims you can't substantiate rather than relying on a customer's generous interpretation — "locally sourced" without backup is a common enforcement trigger precisely because it sounds specific but often isn't.
  5. Train front-of-house staff not to add unverified claims verbally that aren't on the printed menu — a server telling a customer fish is "caught this morning" without basis creates the same legal exposure as printing it.
