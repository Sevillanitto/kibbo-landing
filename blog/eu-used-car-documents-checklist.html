Buying a Used Car in the EU: Which Documents Should You Check Before Paying?

A used car sale involves more paperwork than most buyers check. Here's what each document actually proves — and what it doesn't.

Vehicle registration certificate

Confirms the vehicle's technical details and, in most member states, the current registered keeper. Check the name matches the seller, and that the VIN printed on the document matches the VIN stamped on the vehicle itself — a mismatch is one of the clearest signs of a cloned or misrepresented vehicle.

Proof of ownership vs. proof of registration

In several EU countries these are separate documents (or the registration certificate alone isn't conclusive proof of ownership) — don't assume having the registration paper means the seller is legally entitled to sell. Ask for the original purchase invoice or prior sale contract where available.

Roadworthiness inspection records

Every EU member state runs a periodic technical inspection scheme (equivalent to the UK's MOT), though the name, interval and format vary by country. Ask for the inspection history and cross-check the mileage recorded at each inspection against the odometer today — inconsistent progression is a mileage-fraud signal.

Maintenance history and the purchase contract

A service book alone can be incomplete or altered — invoices from named workshops are stronger evidence of real maintenance. The purchase contract itself should state the price, mileage, condition, and whether the sale is between two private individuals or involves a professional trader, since this materially changes your legal protections.
