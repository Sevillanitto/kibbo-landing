"European Master" or Private Certificate? How to Check What a Degree Is Actually Worth

A "Master" marketed as European or Bologna-accredited can still be an unofficial private qualification, with no legal standing for doctoral entry or public sector exams — and only your own country's official register will tell you the difference.

The Marketing Language That Means Nothing Legally

Terms like "European Master," "accredited by the European Higher Education Area," or "Bologna-compliant" are frequently used in private academy marketing without any binding legal meaning attached to them. The European Higher Education Area (EHEA), created by the Bologna Process, is a framework for cooperation and comparability between countries' official higher education systems — it does not itself accredit institutions or issue qualifications, and referencing it in marketing copy doesn't make a private, non-official qualification into an official one.

The distinction that actually matters legally in most EU countries is between an "official" (oficial) qualification — registered in the country's national register of universities and degrees, carrying legal effects like access to further official study or eligibility for regulated professions — and a "private" or "own" (propio) qualification issued directly by an institution without that registration, which typically carries no automatic legal recognition outside the issuing institution itself.

Checking the Real Status — Country by Country

Because higher education accreditation remains a national competence in the EU (not centralized at the EU level), verification has to happen through your specific country's own official register, not a single EU-wide database. In Spain, for example, official degrees are recorded in the RUCT (Registro de Universidades, Centros y Títulos), accessible through the Ministry's electronic portal. Other EU countries maintain their own equivalent national registers. The European Qualifications Framework (EQF) is a separate, useful reference tool for comparing qualification levels across countries once you've confirmed a qualification is genuinely official — but it isn't itself a searchable accreditation database, so don't expect to verify legitimacy through it directly.

Why This Matters Beyond Prestige

An unofficial "own" master's degree may still be a genuinely valuable, well-taught program — the distinction isn't automatically about quality. What it does typically lack is specific legal effects: access to official doctoral programs that require an official master's as a prerequisite, eligibility for public sector competitive exams (oposiciones) that require an official qualification, and automatic recognition in other EU countries under mutual recognition frameworks that apply specifically to official qualifications.

Verification Steps

  1. Search the exact program name in your country's official qualifications register before enrolling, not after.
  2. If the program is genuinely official, note the specific register entry — this is your proof if the qualification's status is ever questioned later.
  3. If you need the qualification specifically for doctoral access or a public sector exam, confirm directly with the receiving institution or examining body that this specific program satisfies their requirement — don't rely solely on the marketing claim.
  4. Be skeptical of "European" or "Bologna" branding used as a substitute for a specific, checkable official registration claim.
