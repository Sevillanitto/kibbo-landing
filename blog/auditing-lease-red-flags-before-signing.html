Auditing a Rental Contract: How to Spot Legal Red Flags in Penalty Clauses, Renewal Fees, and Entry Terms

Most lease disputes trace back to a clause the tenant noticed but didn't push back on before signing — here's what to actually look for.

Late Payment Penalty Clauses

Look specifically at how a late fee is structured, not just whether one exists. A flat, reasonable one-time fee is standard practice almost everywhere. A fee that compounds daily with no cap, or that's calculated as a percentage far above what similar penalty clauses typically look like in your jurisdiction, is worth flagging — many jurisdictions cap or otherwise regulate penalty clauses, and an uncapped daily-compounding fee is one of the more common ways a lease crosses that line.

Hidden Renewal and Administration Fees

Read the renewal clause specifically for whether the terms of a future renewal are actually defined, or left to the landlord's discretion at the time. A clause stating the lease "automatically renews under terms to be determined by the landlord" gives you no way to know in advance what you'd actually be agreeing to. Similarly, watch for administration, processing, or "convenience" fees that aren't disclosed as part of the headline rent figure — these are exactly the kind of costs that transparency-focused fee regulations, like the FTC's 2025 rule requiring platforms and lodging providers to disclose total pricing upfront, were designed to target in the adjacent short-term rental space, and the same logic applies to long-term lease fees that only surface after you've committed.

Entry and Access Clauses

A lease that allows the landlord to enter "at any time" or "without notice" is one of the most consistent red flags across jurisdictions — most tenancy laws require a minimum notice period for routine landlord entry, and a clause purporting to waive that requirement is often unenforceable even if signed. Check specifically whether the clause distinguishes between routine entry (inspections, showings, repairs — which should require notice) and genuine emergencies (which reasonably don't).

Unjustified Blanket Exclusions

A blanket "no pets" clause with no exceptions process is common and generally enforceable in most places, but watch for one paired with a non-refundable "pet fee" charged even where no pet is present, or a clause that conflicts with legally protected assistance animal accommodations where those protections apply. The specific enforceability of a pet exclusion, and any required accommodation exceptions, varies significantly by jurisdiction.

What This Means Practically

  • Read every clause, not just the ones your landlord specifically walks you through — the fine print is where most problems live.
  • Flag any clause with an undefined future term ("to be determined," "at landlord's discretion") — get it defined in writing before signing, not after.
  • Question any fee not clearly disclosed as part of the headline rent figure.
  • If a clause seems to waive a right you believe is legally protected, raise it in writing before signing rather than assuming it's unenforceable and safe to ignore.
