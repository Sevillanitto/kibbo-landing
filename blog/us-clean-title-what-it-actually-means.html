Buying a Used Car With a Clean Title: What That Actually Tells You

"Clean title" sounds like a guarantee the car has never been damaged. It isn't one — it's a narrower, specific statement about paperwork, and it leaves real gaps a title alone will never show you.

What "clean" actually certifies

A clean title means the state hasn't applied a brand — like salvage, rebuilt, flood, or junk — to that specific title record. It says nothing about repair quality, prior accidents that didn't trigger an insurance total-loss declaration, or damage repaired without ever going through insurance at all. A car can be genuinely clean-titled and still have been in a serious, professionally repaired accident.

The "title washing" gap

Because branding rules and enforcement vary by state, a vehicle salvage-branded in one state has sometimes been re-registered in a state with weaker tracking, emerging with a clean title there — a practice known as title washing. This is exactly the gap the National Motor Vehicle Title Information System (NMVTIS) was built to close, since it pulls data from all participating states, insurers, and salvage yards into one federally mandated system rather than relying on any single state's records alone.

Where to actually check, not just the title itself

  • NMVTIS (vehiclehistory.gov) — the official, DOJ-backed source; search for an Approved NMVTIS Data Provider, since individual consumers can't get a report directly from providers like Carfax or Experian, which supply dealerships only
  • NICB VINCheck — a free check specifically for stolen or salvage/flood-damaged status reported by participating insurers
  • NHTSA recalls — check the VIN for any open safety recall never repaired
  • Maintenance records — request the full service history, not a summary, and check it against the mileage shown

Mileage: the number that should match everything else

The federal Truth in Mileage Act requires a written odometer disclosure at the time of sale for most vehicles under ten years old — but vehicles over ten years old are actually exempted from this specific title disclosure requirement, despite NHTSA's own data showing more odometer fraud in this older category, not less. Compare the current odometer reading against service and inspection records over time; a mileage history that doesn't progress consistently is a real warning sign regardless of what the title itself shows.

How to actually verify what you're buying

  1. Run the VIN through NMVTIS via an Approved Data Provider, and separately through NICB's free VINCheck.
  2. Request the full maintenance history and compare recorded mileage at each service against the current reading.
  3. Check for any open NHTSA recall on the specific VIN.
  4. Get an independent pre-purchase inspection — a title, however clean, is not a substitute for physically checking the car.
  5. Ask the seller directly about any accident history, even one that didn't result in an insurance total-loss claim, since this wouldn't show up on the title at all.

What this means practically

  • A clean title confirms the absence of a specific state-applied brand — it does not confirm the vehicle was never in an accident.
  • Title washing across state lines is a real, documented gap that NMVTIS specifically exists to close — check it rather than relying on a single state's title alone.
  • Vehicles over ten years old are exempt from federal odometer title disclosure requirements, despite carrying higher documented rates of odometer fraud.
  • A title check and an independent physical inspection answer different questions — you need both, not one instead of the other.
