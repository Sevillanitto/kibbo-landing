Accidental Doping in Australia: How Contaminated Supplements End Athletes' Careers

Under anti-doping rules, it doesn't matter how a banned substance got into your body. A contaminated pre-workout is a legitimate defence for a lighter sanction — it's never a shield from one entirely.

Strict liability: the rule that catches unintentional users

Under the World Anti-Doping Code's strict liability principle, athletes are responsible for any prohibited substance found in their body, regardless of how it got there. Sport Integrity Australia has warned that prohibited substances sometimes appear on labels under unfamiliar botanical or chemical names — a product listing an ingredient you don't recognise doesn't mean it's safe from a doping perspective.

How common is contamination, really?

A 2025 survey analysis of the Australian online sports supplement marketplace found a 35% prevalence rate for products containing a WADA Prohibited Substance — meaning an athlete buying an uncertified product online faced roughly a one-in-three chance the product contained something banned. This wasn't limited to obscure or poorly branded products; scheduled substances including SARMs and beta-2 agonists like higenamine turned up across the sample.

Higenamine: a real, documented example

Higenamine, a beta-2 agonist naturally occurring in some plants, has no history of safe use as a food in Australia and hasn't been adequately tested for safety in humans via oral exposure. It's been banned in Australian supplements since a TGA ruling in December 2020, and separately banned in sport by WADA. It has genuinely turned up in Australian sports supplements sold under names that obscure its presence, making label-reading alone an unreliable protection.

What HASTA and Informed Sport actually do differently

HASTA (Human and Supplement Testing Australia), developed with Sport Integrity Australia, and the international Informed Sport program both test sports supplements specifically against the WADA prohibited substance list, publishing certified products together with their tested batch numbers. This batch-specific approach matters because contamination can vary between production runs of the same product — a general "clean formula" claim without batch-level testing doesn't rule out cross-contamination in a specific bottle.

How to reduce your own risk

  1. Favor products certified by HASTA or Informed Sport specifically, and check the batch number on your actual product against the certified batch list.
  2. Be especially cautious with any product containing unfamiliar botanical-sounding ingredient names — research each one individually rather than assuming "natural" means safe from a doping standpoint.
  3. Remember that strict liability means a genuine contamination case can still result in a sanction, just potentially a reduced one if you can demonstrate the source.
  4. If you're a drug-tested athlete at any level, treat every new supplement as a real risk decision, not a routine purchase.

What this means practically

  • Roughly a third of uncertified sports supplements sold online in Australia have tested positive for a banned substance in recent survey data — this is a real, current risk, not a rare edge case.
  • A prohibited substance can appear on a label under an unfamiliar botanical name, so recognising every ingredient by its common name isn't a reliable safety check.
  • HASTA and Informed Sport both test at the batch level specifically because contamination risk isn't uniform across a brand's entire production.
  • Strict liability means even genuine contamination is treated as the athlete's responsibility by default — prevention matters more than any after-the-fact defence.
