Non-Covered Items: What Airlines Will Never Pay For in Checked Baggage

Statutory liability caps only matter for items the airline was ever obligated to cover in the first place — and buried in every Contract of Carriage is a list of things that don't qualify no matter how the bag was lost.

The Myth

"If I pack camera gear, jewelry, or cash inside my checked suitcase and the airline loses the bag, they have to pay for everything inside, up to the statutory limit."

The statutory caps — $4,700 domestically, 1,519 SDR internationally — set a ceiling on what an airline can be liable for. They don't guarantee every category of item is covered at all.

The Reality

Buried inside every airline's Conditions of Carriage — the binding contract you accept the moment you buy a ticket — are strict exemption clauses. Airlines explicitly exclude certain high-value, fragile, or essential personal items from checked baggage liability entirely. If you pack an excluded item in a checked bag and that bag is lost, damaged, or stolen, the airline is contractually permitted to reject reimbursement for that specific item, even while paying out for everything else in the bag that isn't excluded.

The Hard Data: Standard Carrier Exclusions

While the statutory frameworks (DOT rules and the Montreal Convention) govern the overall liability ceiling, airlines contractually restrict what belongs in the cargo hold in the first place. Commonly excluded categories include:

  • Cash, currency, and negotiable securities
  • Jewelry, precious metals, and luxury watches
  • Consumer electronics — laptops, tablets, cameras, smartphones
  • Prescription medications and critical medical equipment
  • Passports, visas, and legal identification documents
  • Irreplaceable heirlooms, antiques, and original artwork
  • Keys — house keys, car keys

The Regulatory Limitation on These Exclusions

International flights (Montreal Convention): Article 17 of MC99 establishes strict liability for lost checked bags. While airlines attempt to contractually exclude valuables, international courts have in some cases overridden carrier exclusions where the airline accepted the checked bag without warning the passenger of the exclusion, or without offering an Excess Value Declaration at check-in.

U.S. domestic flights (DOT rules): the DOT prohibits carriers from completely excluding liability for fragile or valuable items unless those exclusions are clearly disclosed in the airline's published terms of service — a disclosure requirement, not a guarantee the item is covered.

Real Cases: The Cost of Packing Valuables in Cargo

Case 1: The Missing Jewelry Box

The scenario: A passenger packs $4,000 in fine jewelry inside a checked suit bag on a domestic flight. The bag arrives, but the jewelry box inside has been opened and emptied.

The airline's response: The carrier denies liability for the stolen jewelry, citing its Contract of Carriage requirement that jewelry be kept in carry-on baggage.

The reality: Because the passenger packed the valuables in cargo despite an explicit contractual exclusion, recovering funds from the airline directly is very difficult without third-party travel insurance or clear proof of TSA or handler theft specifically.

Case 2: Prescription Medication Stranded in Transit

The scenario: A traveler packs essential prescription medication inside checked luggage on an international flight. The bag is delayed for 5 days during a connecting layover.

The outcome: Beyond the health impact of the delay, the passenger pays out of pocket for emergency local prescription refills. The airline reimburses basic clothing expenses under the delayed-baggage rules, but refuses to cover medical consultation costs or local replacement medication, citing the same checked-baggage exclusions.

The Protocol: Protecting High-Value Items Before You Fly

  1. Keep critical items in your personal carry-on. Medication, keys, passports, electronics, cash, and high-value jewelry should never go in checked luggage.
  2. Use an Excess Value Declaration if you must check high-value items. Request this at the ticket counter before checking the bag — paying a nominal fee raises the carrier's liability ceiling specifically for that piece of luggage.
  3. Consider third-party scheduled property insurance. A specialized travel insurance policy, or a personal-property endorsement on your home or renters insurance, can cover high-value jewelry, camera equipment, or sports gear explicitly for transit loss.
  4. If you're forced to gate-check a carry-on, remove all electronics, medications, keys, and valuables before handing the bag to ramp personnel — a gate-check is functionally the same as checked baggage for liability purposes.
