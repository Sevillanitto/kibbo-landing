A Business Didn't Do What the Contract Promised: What Can You Do in Australia?

You paid for one thing. The business delivered another. In Australia, you often have two separate legal angles available at once — your specific contract terms, and the ACL's consumer guarantees — and using both together strengthens your position.

Two overlapping protections, not one

A breach of contract claim looks at what your specific contract actually promised versus what was delivered. Separately, the Australian Consumer Law's consumer guarantees apply automatically to most consumer purchases of goods and services, regardless of what any contract or store policy says — guarantees like acceptable quality, fitness for purpose, and matching the description given. These guarantees can't be excluded or limited by a contract term, and they apply even where a specific breach claim might be harder to establish.

Identify the specific obligation first

Whichever angle you pursue, start by identifying precisely what was promised — quote the exact contract clause, or the specific representation made about the goods or service — and compare it to what actually happened. A vague sense that the service was disappointing is much harder to act on than "the contract specified X and I received Y."

Document before you complain

  • The contract or agreement itself, and any related order confirmation or invoice
  • A clear, dated timeline of what happened and when
  • Photos or other evidence of the actual goods or work delivered
  • Any communications where the business acknowledged the problem
  • Your actual financial loss, itemized with receipts or estimates where possible

The complaint escalation path

  1. Raise the issue directly with the business first, in writing, and give them a genuine opportunity to fix it.
  2. If unresolved, escalate to your state or territory's fair trading or consumer affairs body — this is often the fastest, free first step beyond the business itself.
  3. For matters involving misleading conduct, unfair terms, or broader market conduct concerns, the ACCC is the relevant national body, though it generally doesn't resolve individual disputes directly.
  4. If the amount involved justifies it and direct resolution fails, your state or territory's civil and administrative tribunal can hear the matter — this is where citing specific ACL consumer guarantee violations, not just a general complaint, strengthens your case significantly.

Why citing the ACL specifically helps

A dispute framed only as "I'm not happy with the service" is harder to act on than one framed as "this fails the acceptable quality guarantee under the Australian Consumer Law." Consumer guarantee rights exist independently of any store return policy or contract term claiming otherwise — citing them specifically, by name, tends to get a faster, more substantive response from a business than a general complaint does.

What this means practically

  • You often have two separate legal bases at once — your specific contract terms and the ACL's automatic consumer guarantees — and citing both strengthens your position.
  • Consumer guarantees can't be excluded by a contract term or store policy, regardless of what it says.
  • Document the specific promised obligation, what actually happened, and your loss before raising the issue formally.
  • Your state or territory's fair trading body and civil tribunal are the practical escalation path if direct resolution with the business fails.
