Spain's Food Waste Law: The Free Doggy Bag Obligation Explained

Since April 3, 2025, every restaurant in Spain — except all-you-can-eat buffets — must let customers take home their leftovers in a free, sustainable container. A written prevention plan became fully enforceable in April 2026.

Ley 1/2025: Two Different Compliance Dates

Spain's Ley 1/2025, de 1 de abril, de prevención de las pérdidas y el desperdicio alimentario was published in the BOE on April 2, 2025, making it the country's first dedicated legal framework governing food loss and waste across the entire food chain — from primary producer through to the final restaurant table. The law sets two separate compliance milestones that are easy to conflate: the customer-facing leftovers obligation took effect April 3, 2025 (Article 8), while the internal prevention plan requirement (Article 6) had a transitional period that concluded April 2, 2026 — meaning that requirement is now fully and currently enforceable, not merely upcoming.

The Leftovers Obligation, in Practice

Every restaurant and hospitality establishment in Spain — regardless of size — must visibly inform customers of their right to take home food or drink they haven't consumed, and must provide, free of charge, a container suitable for food contact that is reusable or easily recyclable. Single-use plastic containers can still be charged for separately under Spain's existing waste law (Ley 7/2022), but the food-container obligation itself carries no charge. The one explicit carve-out: all-you-can-eat buffets and similar formats are exempt, since food availability there isn't portion-limited in the same way, which would otherwise create an unworkable loophole.

The Prevention Plan Requirement

Beyond the visible customer-facing obligation, every establishment must maintain its own documented food loss and waste prevention plan, following a mandatory hierarchy of priorities for handling surplus food (broadly: prevent surplus first, then prioritize human consumption and donation over any other use, including animal feed or energy recovery, as a last resort). The plan must include a self-diagnosis of where losses or waste risk occurs in the business's own operations, concrete measures to reduce it, staff training on waste-reduction practices, and monitoring indicators. A restaurant operating in more than one Autonomous Community should build a single integrated plan that accounts for any region-specific requirements rather than maintaining entirely separate plans.

Penalties

Enforcement and sanctions are delegated to the Autonomous Communities, so exact procedures vary regionally, but the national penalty framework sets three tiers: minor infractions (such as failing to visibly inform customers of the leftovers right) starting around €2,000; serious infractions (such as lacking a prevention plan altogether) ranging roughly from €2,001 to €60,000; and very serious infractions (such as deliberate destruction of food) reaching up to €500,000, with repeat violations within a two-year window escalating toward the higher end of that range.

Compliance Checklist

  1. Display clear, visible signage (menu note or physical sign) informing customers of their right to take home leftovers — this alone avoids the lowest tier of fine.
  2. Stock a free, food-safe, reusable or easily recyclable container as standard — confirm it meets food-contact material rules.
  3. Draft and keep on file a written prevention plan following the legal hierarchy of priorities, including a donation pathway for safe surplus food.
  4. Train front-of-house staff to proactively offer the container rather than waiting for the customer to ask.
  5. Confirm your Autonomous Community's specific enforcement contact and any additional regional requirements layered on top of the national law.
