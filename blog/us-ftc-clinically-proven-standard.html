What "Clinically Proven" Is Actually Supposed to Mean, Per the FTC

A fat burner or nootropic calls itself "clinically proven." The FTC has a specific, demanding definition of what that phrase should require — and most marketing claims that use it don't come close.

The guidance that set the modern bar

On December 20, 2022, the Federal Trade Commission issued its Health Products Compliance Guidance, replacing a 1998 guide and expanding its scope to essentially all health-related product advertising, not just dietary supplements. It defines the standard health claims must meet: "competent and reliable scientific evidence."

What that phrase actually requires

The FTC defines competent and reliable scientific evidence as tests, analyses, research, or studies conducted and evaluated in an objective manner by qualified experts in the relevant field, using methods generally accepted as producing accurate results. As a general matter, the guidance states that substantiating a health-related benefit will need to take the form of randomized, controlled human clinical trials (RCTs) to meet this standard.

What does NOT count, according to the FTC

  • Animal studies or in vitro (test tube/lab) studies
  • Epidemiological or observational studies, except in limited cases where experts agree they're an acceptable substitute and RCTs aren't feasible
  • Anecdotal customer experiences or testimonials
  • General public health recommendations or advisories
  • A study that only tested the individual ingredient in isolation, at a different dose, rather than the actual finished product being sold

What a genuinely well-designed study needs

The FTC looks for a treatment and control group, proper randomization, double-blinding, a clearly defined research protocol set out in advance, statistically significant results, and results that are also clinically meaningful — meaning the effect is large enough to actually matter to the person using the product, not just large enough to be statistically detectable. The guidance also warns against "p-hacking": running many statistical tests after the fact until something looks significant, rather than testing a specific, pre-defined hypothesis.

How to sanity-check a "clinically proven" claim yourself

  1. Look for a specific, named, citable study — not just the phrase "clinical studies show."
  2. Check whether the study tested this exact finished product, at this exact dose, rather than just the general ingredient.
  3. Check whether the study had a control group and was randomized and blinded — a small internal study with no control group doesn't meet the FTC's bar.
  4. Be skeptical of "scientists agree" or "clinically proven" language with zero citation offered anywhere, even in fine print.
  5. Remember that the number of studies matters less than the quality of even one well-designed study — one solid RCT can outweigh several weak ones.

What this means practically

  • "Clinically proven" is meant to signal randomized, controlled human trials — not an internal company test or a study of the raw ingredient alone.
  • A citation that only tested the ingredient generically, not the actual product and dose being sold, doesn't meet the standard the FTC expects.
  • Testimonials, before/after photos, and animal studies are explicitly not considered adequate substantiation on their own.
  • If a specific, verifiable study can't be found behind a "clinically proven" claim, treat the claim with real skepticism regardless of how confidently it's marketed.
